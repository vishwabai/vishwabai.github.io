🥾

The one-sentence version

Imagine you're dropped on a hill in thick fog and told "find the lowest point." You can't see the map — but you can feel which way the ground tilts under your boots. Optimization is the rulebook for using that local "tilt" information to find the best spot, step by step, even when you can't see the whole landscape at once.

A problem you actually care about

Every time your maps app finds the fastest route, your music app tunes a recommendation, or an AI model "learns" from examples, the same machine is running underneath: define a score for "how good is this answer," then use the local slope of that score to walk toward something better. Training a large language model is, mechanically, millions of tiny downhill steps on an enormous landscape of numbers — the exact same idea you're about to learn on a single hill.

CH 01 THE FIRST IDEA
⏱️ 8 min

Better vs. Best

Before you can find the "best" anything, you need a single number that tells you how good a candidate answer is. Without that number, "optimize" is meaningless — there's nothing to compare.

$$ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta} \; f(\theta) $$

Read this as: "$\theta^*$ (the best answer) is whichever $\theta$ makes the objective function $f(\theta)$ as small as possible." For our omelette, $\theta$ is the cooking time and $f(\theta)$ is "badness" (negative yumminess).

What you think

Optimization means trying different answers until one "feels right," like guessing a password.

What's actually true

Optimization means defining a score (called the objective function) for every possible answer, then using the shape of that score — not guesswork — to systematically walk toward a better one.

⛳

Feynman analogy #1 — golf score

In golf, lower is better — your score is a number you want to minimize. In the high jump, higher is better — you want to maximize. Both sports have exactly one thing in common: a single number that tells you, instantly, whether one attempt beat another. That number is the objective function. Optimization never argues about "good" in the abstract — it always reduces the question to "is this number better than that number?"

🍳

Feynman analogy #2 — the omelette

Say you're optimizing "how long to cook an omelette." Too little time: runny. Too much time: rubbery. There's a sweet spot in the middle. Plot "yumminess" against "cooking time" and you get a hill, not a straight line — yumminess goes up, peaks, then comes back down. That hill shape is the objective function for this problem, and the peak is what you're hunting for.

The objective function for "omelette yumminess" — a hill with one best point.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

People often confuse "the objective function" with "the answer." It's neither — it's the ruler you use to measure answers. The omelette's cooking time is the answer you're searching for; "yumminess" is the ruler. Mixing these up is the single most common reason beginners get lost in every chapter that follows, so it's worth re-reading this paragraph twice.

Reveal the why: why does the goal switch between "minimize" and "maximize"?

It's purely a labeling choice — and you can always flip one into the other. Maximizing "yumminess" is identical to minimizing "negative yumminess." That's why almost every optimization textbook only ever talks about minimizing: any maximization problem can be rewritten as a minimization problem just by flipping the sign of the score.

Teach it back

In your own words: what is an objective function, and why do you need one before you can optimize anything?

An objective function is the single number that turns "which answer is better?" into a comparison of two numbers. You need it first because optimization has no other way to judge progress — every later technique (slopes, gradients, constraints) is just a smarter way of moving across the score this function defines.

Worked example — finding the omelette optimum exactly

Suppose careful measurement gives the badness function $f(t) = (t-4)^2 + 1$, where $t$ is cooking time in minutes. To find $t^*$:

  1. Differentiate: $f'(t) = 2(t-4)$.
  2. Set the derivative to zero (flat ground — the candidate optimum): $2(t-4)=0 \Rightarrow t=4$.
  3. Check it's a minimum, not a maximum: $f''(t) = 2 > 0$ everywhere, so the curve is convex (bowl-shaped) and $t=4$ is guaranteed to be the global minimum.
  4. Confirm: $f(4) = 0 + 1 = 1$, the lowest possible badness.

Four minutes, badness score of 1 — no guessing required, just the three-step recipe: differentiate, set to zero, check the second derivative's sign.

Key takeaway

Every optimization problem starts with one decision: what single number am I trying to push up or down? Everything else in this course is just smarter and smarter ways of moving along that number's shape.

CH 02 ONE NEW IDEA
⏱️ 10 min

Slope: the Compass of Change

Now that you have a score to chase, you need a way to know which direction improves it without checking every possible answer. That's the job of the slope.

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

In plain words: take a tiny step $h$ forward, see how much the height $f(x)$ changed, divide by the size of the step, then imagine shrinking that step to nothing. What's left is the slope at exactly that point.

What you think

"Derivative" is some scary symbol-pushing thing — or worse, it's confused with the area under a curve.

What's actually true

A derivative is just the steepness of the ground right where you're standing — a single number that says "tilting up" (positive), "tilting down" (negative), or "flat" (zero). Nothing more mystical than that.

🦶

Feynman analogy #1 — blindfolded on a hill

Close your eyes on a grassy slope. You don't need a map to know which way is "downhill from here" — your feet feel the tilt. The derivative is that feeling, written as a number: how much the height changes for a tiny step forward.

🚗

Feynman analogy #2 — the speedometer

Your car's odometer tells you total distance (like the function's value). Your speedometer tells you how fast that distance is changing right now (like the derivative). You never need to know the whole trip to read the speedometer — just this instant. That's exactly what a derivative measures about any curve: not the trip, just this instant's rate of change.

The curve is f(x) = (x − 50)² / 200 + 1. As you slide, the dashed amber line is the tangent — the straight-line approximation of the curve right at that point. Its steepness is the derivative's value.

$$ f(x) = \frac{(x-50)^2}{200} + 1 $$
$$ f'(x) = \frac{x-50}{100} $$

Using the power rule, $\frac{d}{dx}(x-50)^2 = 2(x-50)$, and dividing by 200 gives $f'(x)=\frac{2(x-50)}{200}=\frac{x-50}{100}$. Plug in any x from the slider above and you'll get exactly the slope shown in the readout.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

The phrase "rate of change" trips people up because it sounds like it needs two points in time. It doesn't — a derivative is defined at a single instant, by imagining a step so tiny it's basically zero. Don't picture "before and after"; picture "right here, right now, leaning."

Reveal the why: why does a slope of zero matter so much?

A slope of zero means the ground is flat right where you're standing — you're not currently tilted up or down. That's exactly the signature of a hilltop, a valley floor, or a flat plateau. Every optimization algorithm in this course is, underneath, searching for the spot where the slope hits zero.

Teach it back

A derivative tells you the steepness of a curve at one exact point — whether you're tilted up, down, or flat, and by how much. A slope of zero is significant because it marks the only places a smooth curve can have a peak, a valley, or a flat plateau — which is exactly what optimization is hunting for.

Worked example — the limit definition, by hand

Let's derive $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2$ directly from the limit definition, with no shortcuts:

  1. Write out the difference quotient: $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$.
  2. Expand $(x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$, so the numerator becomes $2xh + h^2$.
  3. Divide by $h$: $\dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h} = 2x + h$.
  4. Let $h \to 0$: only $2x$ survives, so $f'(x) = 2x$.

This is exactly where the "power rule" you may have memorized — bring the exponent down, subtract one from it — actually comes from: $\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x^{2-1} = 2x$, not a magic rule but a shortcut for this limit.

Key takeaway

The derivative is a local compass: a single number, recalculated at every point, that tells you whether stepping forward makes things better or worse — and zero is the universal signal for "you might be at a special point."

CH 03 INTERMEDIATE
⏱️ 12 min

The Gradient: Slope in Many Directions

A road only has one direction to walk: forward or back. A hillside has infinitely many. The gradient is what "slope" becomes when you're not stuck on a line.

$$ \nabla f(\theta) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_n} \right] $$

The gradient $\nabla f$ is just a list of ordinary derivatives, one per direction — each $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_i}$ asks "how steep is it if I only wiggle $\theta_i$ and freeze everything else?" Stack the answers into one vector and you get an arrow pointing uphill.

What you think

"Gradient descent" is some exotic AI-only trick, totally different from the slope you just learned.

What's actually true

The gradient is exactly the same idea as the derivative — "which way is steepest" — just measured in every direction at once and combined into one arrow. Walking the opposite way that arrow points is the entire idea behind gradient descent.

🧭

Feynman analogy #1 — the hiker with two feet

On a 2D hillside (not a road), one foot can feel "steeper this way," the other foot "steeper that way." Combine both feelings into a single arrow pointing in the steepest uphill direction — that arrow is the gradient. Walk exactly opposite to it, and you're guaranteed to be heading downhill as fast as locally possible.

💧

Feynman analogy #2 — water finding its way

Pour water on a hillside. It doesn't consult a map; at every point it simply flows in the steepest downhill direction available right there. Gradient descent is water, simulated with arithmetic.

$$ \theta_{\,t+1} = \theta_t \;-\; \alpha \, \nabla f(\theta_t) $$

The entire gradient descent algorithm in one line: take your current position $\theta_t$, subtract the gradient (scaled by a learning rate $\alpha$), and that's your next position $\theta_{t+1}$. Press "Step" above and watch this exact formula run, one step at a time.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

The minus sign in "θ_new = θ_old − rate × gradient" confuses almost everyone the first time. The gradient points uphill (steepest increase) by definition — so to go downhill, you must walk in the opposite direction, which is why the formula subtracts it. Forgetting that minus sign is the single most common bug in real gradient descent code.

Reveal the why: what happens if you pick a learning rate that's too big?

You'll see in the simulator above — too large a step and θ leaps clean over the valley to the opposite slope, sometimes landing higher than it started. Chapter 6 dedicates itself entirely to this exact problem.

Teach it back

In your own words: how does gradient descent decide which way to move, and why is there a minus sign?

At every point, gradient descent computes the gradient — the direction of steepest increase. Since the goal is usually to minimize, it moves in exactly the opposite direction, scaled by the learning rate. The minus sign is what flips "steepest uphill" into "steepest downhill."

Worked example — one gradient descent step in two dimensions

Let $f(x,y) = x^2 + 2y^2$ and start at $\theta_0 = (3, 2)$ with learning rate $\alpha = 0.1$.

  1. Compute each partial derivative: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 4y$.
  2. Evaluate at $\theta_0$: $\nabla f(3,2) = (2 \cdot 3,\; 4 \cdot 2) = (6, 8)$.
  3. Apply the update rule: $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \alpha \nabla f(\theta_0) = (3,2) - 0.1\,(6,8) = (3-0.6,\; 2-0.8) = (2.4,\; 1.2)$.
  4. Check progress: $f(3,2) = 9+8=17$ and $f(2.4,1.2)=5.76+2.88=8.64$ — the score roughly halved in one step.

Notice the $y$-direction moved further (0.8) than the $x$-direction (0.6) — because the bowl is steeper along $y$ (coefficient 2 vs. 1), the gradient is naturally larger there, and a single learning rate automatically scales the step accordingly in each direction.

Key takeaway

The gradient generalizes "slope" to many directions at once and points uphill. Gradient descent is simply: find that arrow, walk the opposite way, repeat.

CH 04 INTERMEDIATE

Valleys & False Valleys

Following the slope downhill always works locally. The uncomfortable truth is that "downhill from here" doesn't always lead to the lowest point that exists.

$$ f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda) y) \;\le\; \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y), \quad \forall \lambda \in [0,1] $$

This is the formal definition of a convex function: draw a straight line between any two points $x$ and $y$ on the curve, and the curve itself always lies on or below that line. A single round bowl always passes this test; an egg carton never does.

What you think

If gradient descent stops moving, it must have found the best possible answer.

What's actually true

Gradient descent stops when the local slope is zero — but a bumpy landscape can have many flat-bottomed dips. It might have found a minimum, a "local" one, while a deeper "global" minimum sits untouched elsewhere on the map.

🥣

Feynman analogy #1 — marble in a bowl vs. an egg carton

Drop a marble in a single round bowl: no matter where it starts, it always settles at the true bottom. That shape is called convex. Now drop the same marble into an egg carton: it settles into whichever dip it happened to land near — even if a deeper dip exists three slots over. Most real-world landscapes are egg cartons, not bowls.

🏔️

Feynman analogy #2 — fog on a mountain range

In thick fog you can absolutely find the bottom of a valley using nothing but your feet. But you have no way of knowing, from inside that fog, whether a deeper valley exists two ridges over. That uncertainty isn't a bug in your method — it's a fundamental limit of only ever being able to see the ground right around you.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

People assume convexity is rare and exotic. In practice many small, well-posed problems (like the omelette example) are convex bowls — it's specifically high-dimensional, real-world problems (like training a large model) where bumpy "egg carton" landscapes become the norm, which is exactly why later chapters introduce extra tricks to cope with that.

Reveal the why: if local minima are a risk, why does gradient descent still work so well in practice?

Two reasons: first, many practical landscapes are "mostly bowl-shaped" near a good answer even if not perfectly convex everywhere. Second, running the search from several different starting points (like the four marbles above) and keeping the best result found is a simple, surprisingly effective hedge against getting unlucky.

Teach it back

In your own words: what's the difference between a local minimum and a global minimum, and why can't gradient descent always tell which one it found?

A local minimum is just the bottom of whichever dip you're in; a global minimum is the deepest dip on the entire landscape. Gradient descent only ever feels the slope right around its current position, so once it lands somewhere with zero local slope, it has no way to "see" whether a deeper dip exists elsewhere — it can only report that it's stuck, not that it's optimal.

Worked example — testing convexity with the second derivative

Is $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ convex? Let's check, and find its critical points along the way.

  1. First derivative: $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 8x$. Setting it to zero: $4x(x^2-2)=0$, giving three candidates — $x = 0,\ \sqrt{2},\ -\sqrt{2}$.
  2. Second derivative: $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 8$.
  3. Test each candidate's sign of $f''$: at $x=0$, $f''(0) = -8 < 0$ (a local maximum — a hilltop). At $x=\pm\sqrt{2}$, $f''(\sqrt2) = 12(2)-8 = 16 > 0$ (local minima).
  4. Since $f''$ changes sign (negative near 0, positive further out), $f$ is not convex everywhere — it's exactly the "egg carton" shape from this chapter's analogy, with two separate valleys at $x=\pm\sqrt2$, both giving $f(\pm\sqrt2) = 4-8=-4$, tied for the global minimum.

This is precisely why gradient descent starting near $x=0.1$ would slide toward $x=\sqrt2$, while starting near $x=-0.1$ would slide toward $x=-\sqrt2$ — two different starting points, two different (but equally good) local minima.

Key takeaway

"Stopped moving" means "local slope is zero," not "found the best answer in the universe." Convex (bowl-shaped) problems guarantee the two are the same; bumpy real-world problems don't.

Playing by the Rules: Constraints

So far, θ has been free to roam anywhere. Real problems usually come with rules: a budget you can't exceed, a speed limit, a fixed amount of material. Optimizing within a boundary needs one more idea.

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta, \lambda) = f(\theta) + \lambda \, g(\theta) $$ $$ \nabla f(\theta) = -\lambda \, \nabla g(\theta) $$

$g(\theta)=0$ is the rule you must obey (the leash). The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ glues the objective and the constraint together with a multiplier $\lambda$; setting its gradient to zero forces the second equation — the objective's gradient and the constraint's gradient must point in exactly opposite directions. That's the balance you felt in the sandbox below.

What you think

If you're restricted to a boundary, just check its corners — the best answer is always at a corner, like in a simple budget problem.

What's actually true

That's only true when the boundary is made of straight edges. When the boundary is curved (a circle, a smooth limit), the best point is usually found in the curve's interior edge — at the exact spot where your "want to go this way" direction lines up with the boundary's own "this way is allowed" direction.

🐕

Feynman analogy #1 — walking a dog on a leash

You want to reach a treat sitting just out of reach, but your dog's leash is a fixed length, tied to a post. You can't walk straight to the treat — you're stuck on a circle. The best spot on that circle is the one where, if you tried to take one more tiny step toward the treat, the leash itself would be exactly the thing stopping you — not your own laziness. That's the instant your "pull toward the treat" and the leash's "pull toward the post" are perfectly opposed.

⚖️

Feynman analogy #2 — balancing on a tightrope

A tightrope walker isn't trying to go anywhere off the rope — every useful adjustment they make is along the rope. The moment their "I want to move this way" instinct points exactly perpendicular to the rope, they've found the most stable balance available on that rope. Constrained optimization is the same balance, just in numbers instead of physical balance.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

Students often expect the constrained optimum to be the point on the circle closest to the unconstrained best answer. That's only an accident of perfectly circular, perfectly symmetric setups. In general, the balance condition (gradients pointing in exactly opposite, or perfectly aligned, directions) is the real rule — proximity is not.

Reveal the why: what does "Lagrange multiplier" actually mean in plain words?

It's just the exchange rate between "how much better my score gets" and "how much I'd have to loosen the rule to get there." If loosening the leash by one inch would let you get a lot closer to the treat, the multiplier is large; if it would barely help, it's small. The formal machinery is just bookkeeping for that trade-off.

Teach it back

In your own words: why isn't the constrained optimum simply the closest point on the boundary to the unconstrained best answer?

Because "closest point" only measures distance, while the real optimum balances the objective's pull against the boundary's own shape. The true constrained optimum is wherever your desired direction of improvement is exactly canceled by the boundary's allowed direction of movement — which can sit anywhere on the boundary, not necessarily the nearest spot.

Worked example — solving a constrained optimum exactly

Minimize $f(x,y) = x^2+y^2$ (distance-squared from the origin) subject to staying on the line $g(x,y) = x+y-4 = 0$.

  1. Form the Lagrangian: $\mathcal{L}(x,y,\lambda) = x^2+y^2+\lambda(x+y-4)$.
  2. Take partial derivatives and set each to zero: $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x}=2x+\lambda=0$, $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y}=2y+\lambda=0$, $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda}=x+y-4=0$.
  3. From the first two equations: $x = -\lambda/2$ and $y=-\lambda/2$, so $x=y$.
  4. Substitute into the constraint: $x+x-4=0 \Rightarrow x=2$, so $y=2$ too, and $\lambda=-4$.
  5. The constrained optimum is $(2,2)$, with $f(2,2)=8$ — exactly the point on the line closest to the origin, found without ever checking an "edge" because this constraint has no edges.

$\lambda=-4$ is the exchange rate: nudging the constraint from $x+y=4$ to $x+y=4.01$ would change the optimal score by roughly $\lambda \times 0.01 = -0.04$ — a slightly looser budget buys you a slightly better score, exactly as the leash analogy predicted.

📐 Going further: what if the rule is "at most," not "exactly"? (KKT conditions)

A leash tied to a post (equality, $g(\theta)=0$) is one kind of rule. A fenced yard you merely can't leave (inequality, $g(\theta)\le 0$) is another — and it behaves differently, because sometimes the optimum sits safely inside the fence, away from the boundary entirely. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions generalize the Lagrangian to handle this:

$$ \nabla f(\theta) + \mu \nabla g(\theta) = 0, \qquad \mu \ge 0, \qquad \mu \, g(\theta) = 0 $$

The last condition, called complementary slackness, is the key new idea: either the constraint is exactly on its boundary ($g(\theta)=0$) and pulls on the solution with some real force $\mu>0$, or it's slack — the unconstrained optimum already lies safely inside the fence — in which case $\mu=0$ and the constraint exerts no pull at all. The leash from the diagram above only ever pulls taut; KKT is what lets the math notice when it doesn't need to.

Key takeaway

Constrained optimization isn't "ignore the rule and check corners" — it's finding the precise spot on the boundary where pushing toward a better score and staying inside the rule become perfectly, exactly opposed.

CH 06 ADVANCED
⏱️ 12 min

Taking Smarter Steps: Learning Rate & Momentum

Knowing which direction to step (the gradient) is only half the problem. How big a step you take changes everything about whether you arrive — or overshoot, or crawl forever.

$$ v_{t+1} = \beta v_t + \nabla f(\theta_t) \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha v_{t+1} $$

$v$ is the "velocity" — a running average of recent gradients. $\beta$ (usually around 0.9) controls how much of the old velocity survives each step. When $\beta=0$ this collapses exactly back to plain gradient descent; as $\beta \to 1$, the skateboarder carries more and more of their previous speed through bumps.

What you think

A smaller learning rate is always the "safe" choice, so when in doubt, shrink it.

What's actually true

Too small a step wastes enormous time and can get permanently trapped in tiny, shallow dips that a slightly bigger step would have rolled straight through. Step size is a balance, not a "smaller is safer" dial — and momentum is the specific tool for rolling through those small dips without needing a risky large step size.

🏃

Feynman analogy #1 — three hikers

An eager hiker takes giant leaps downhill — and sometimes leaps clean over the valley floor into the opposite wall, bouncing back and forth without ever settling. A timid hiker takes inch-long steps — technically always moving the right way, but takes forever and gets exhausted (or stuck) in the first tiny dimple in the ground. The hiker who gets there fastest takes steps sized to the terrain: bigger strides on gentle slopes, smaller ones near the bottom.

🛹

Feynman analogy #2 — a skateboarder's momentum

A skateboarder rolling fast through a half-pipe doesn't stop in every tiny crack in the pavement — their built-up speed carries them through. Momentum-based optimization works the same way: it remembers some of its previous direction and "rolls through" small bumps that would have trapped a step that resets to zero speed every single move.

Worked example — three steps of momentum, by hand

Take $f'(\theta) = \theta$ (so the minimum is at $\theta=0$), start at $\theta_0=10$, $v_0=0$, with $\alpha=0.1$ and momentum $\beta=0.9$.

  1. Step 1: $v_1 = 0.9(0) + f'(10) = 10$. Then $\theta_1 = 10 - 0.1(10) = 9.0$.
  2. Step 2: $v_2 = 0.9(10) + f'(9.0) = 9 + 9 = 18$. Then $\theta_2 = 9.0 - 0.1(18) = 7.2$.
  3. Step 3: $v_3 = 0.9(18) + f'(7.2) = 16.2 + 7.2 = 23.4$. Then $\theta_3 = 7.2 - 0.1(23.4) = 4.86$.

Compare the per-step drop: $-1.0$, then $-1.8$, then $-2.34$ — each step moves further than the last, even though the gradient itself is shrinking as $\theta$ approaches zero. That accelerating progress is the accumulated velocity $v$ at work; plain gradient descent with the same $\alpha=0.1$ would only ever move $-1.0,\ -0.9,\ -0.81$ — decelerating, not accelerating.

Key takeaway

Step size is a balance between speed and stability, not a "smaller is safer" dial. Momentum gives you some of the benefits of a bigger step (rolling through small bumps) without the full risk of overshooting.

Using Curvature: Newton's Method

Gradient descent only ever asks "which way is downhill?" It never asks "how sharply does the ground curve here?" Knowing the curvature lets you take a far smarter, often much faster step.

What you think

The only way to speed up an optimizer is to fiddle with the learning rate by trial and error.

What's actually true

The second derivative — how the slope itself is changing — tells you exactly how far you can safely step before the slope flattens out. Using it lets an algorithm choose its own step size automatically, instead of you guessing one.

🎯

Feynman analogy #1 — parking a car

If you're far from a parking spot, you turn the wheel hard. As the car gets close, you straighten out, because you can feel how quickly your trajectory is curving toward the spot. Plain gradient descent is like always turning the wheel by the same fixed amount regardless of how close you are. Newton's method is like a driver who feels the curvature and adjusts the turn size automatically — big correction far away, tiny correction once you're nearly parked.

🏹

Feynman analogy #2 — an archer who learns from the wind

A first arrow tells you not just "too far left," but, if you pay attention, how quickly "left" is changing as you adjust your aim. A first-time archer (gradient descent) corrects by a fixed amount each shot. An archer who also tracks how their correction is curving (Newton's method) can often land the very next arrow dead center, jumping straight to the right answer in far fewer shots — when the curvature estimate is good.

$$ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{f'(\theta_t)}{f''(\theta_t)} $$

Compare this to plain gradient descent, $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha f'(\theta_t)$. Newton's method replaces the hand-picked learning rate $\alpha$ with $\frac{1}{f''(\theta_t)}$ — the step size is computed automatically from the curvature, $f''$, the second derivative.

Reveal the why: where does this formula come from? (Taylor expansion derivation)

Approximate $f$ near $\theta_t$ with a quadratic (second-order Taylor) model: $f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_t) + f'(\theta_t)(\theta-\theta_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(\theta_t)(\theta-\theta_t)^2$. This is just a parabola that matches the true function's value, slope, and curvature at $\theta_t$. Minimizing this parabola exactly — by differentiating with respect to $\theta$ and setting the result to zero — gives $f'(\theta_t) + f''(\theta_t)(\theta-\theta_t) = 0$, which rearranges directly to $\theta = \theta_t - \frac{f'(\theta_t)}{f''(\theta_t)}$. Newton's method is, in other words, "pretend the function is a parabola right here, and jump straight to that parabola's exact minimum."

⚡ Common Sticking Point

Newton's method looks strictly better in the demo above, so people assume it should always be used. In practice, computing $f''$ — and its high-dimensional cousin, the full matrix of second derivatives called the Hessian — gets extremely expensive when there are millions or billions of parameters, which is exactly the regime large AI models live in. That expense is the entire reason plain gradient descent (and its cheaper cousins) still dominates large-scale machine learning.

Reveal the why: what does the second derivative actually measure?

The first derivative tells you the slope; the second derivative tells you how fast that slope itself is changing. A large positive $f''$ means the curve is tightly bowl-shaped (the slope flattens out quickly), so you can safely take a large, confident step. A small $f''$ means the curve is nearly flat for a long stretch, so a smaller, more cautious step is warranted.

📐 Where the formula comes from: a second-order Taylor expansion

Near the current point $\theta_t$, approximate $f$ with a parabola that matches its value, slope, and curvature:

$$ f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_t) + f'(\theta_t)(\theta-\theta_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(\theta_t)(\theta-\theta_t)^2 $$

This parabola is easy to minimize exactly: differentiate it with respect to $\theta$, set the result to zero, and solve. Doing so gives $\theta = \theta_t - f'(\theta_t)/f''(\theta_t)$ — exactly the Newton's method update. In other words, Newton's method doesn't follow the curve itself downhill; at every step it builds the best local parabola it can, then jumps straight to that parabola's exact minimum.

In many dimensions, $f''$ becomes the Hessian matrix $H$ — a full grid of every second partial derivative $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j}$ — and the update becomes $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - H^{-1}\nabla f(\theta_t)$. Inverting that matrix is exactly the expensive step that makes Newton's method impractical once $\theta$ has millions of entries.

Teach it back

In your own words: what extra information does Newton's method use that plain gradient descent ignores, and why isn't it used everywhere?

Newton's method uses the second derivative (curvature) to automatically size its step, instead of relying on a fixed, hand-tuned learning rate. It isn't used everywhere because computing curvature information — the Hessian, in many dimensions — becomes prohibitively expensive once a problem has millions or billions of parameters, which is the typical scale of modern AI models.

Worked example — Newton's method converges in one step on a quadratic

Let $f(\theta) = (\theta-4)^2$, starting at $\theta_0 = 10$.

  1. $f'(\theta) = 2(\theta-4)$, so $f'(10) = 12$.
  2. $f''(\theta) = 2$ (constant, since the function is already a perfect parabola).
  3. Apply the update: $\theta_1 = 10 - \frac{12}{2} = 10 - 6 = 4$.
  4. Check: $f'(4) = 0$ — already at the exact minimum, in a single step.

This isn't a coincidence: since Newton's method exactly minimizes a quadratic approximation, and $f$ here is exactly quadratic, the approximation is perfect and one step suffices. Plain gradient descent with $\alpha=0.1$ would have needed roughly 13 steps to get this close — the curvature information is doing all the extra work.

Key takeaway

Curvature ($f''$) tells an optimizer how confidently to step, letting Newton's method often converge in far fewer iterations — at the cost of needing information that becomes too expensive to compute once a problem has millions of dimensions.

Noisy Steps: Stochastic Gradient Descent

Every gradient you've computed so far used the entire dataset to measure "how wrong am I?" When that dataset has billions of examples, computing one exact gradient before taking a single step becomes absurdly slow. There's a faster — if noisier — way.

What you think

Using a noisy, approximate gradient instead of the true one is a compromise that should make optimization worse.

What's actually true

Taking many cheap, noisy steps using small random batches of data (stochastic gradient descent, or SGD) usually reaches a good answer faster in wall-clock time than taking few, expensive, perfectly accurate steps — and the noise itself can even help the ball hop out of small, shallow local minima.

🗳️

Feynman analogy #1 — an election poll

To know the exact nationwide opinion on an issue, you'd have to survey every single citizen — accurate, but achingly slow. Polling a random sample of a thousand people gives you a noisy, slightly-off estimate almost instantly. Run several quick polls and average their direction, and you converge on the true opinion faster overall than waiting for one perfect, exhaustive survey. A "mini-batch" gradient is exactly this: a quick poll of the data instead of a full census.

🍪

Feynman analogy #2 — tasting cookie dough

You don't need to eat the entire bowl of cookie dough to judge whether it needs more sugar — one representative spoonful (a mini-batch) gives you a noisy but useful estimate, fast enough to adjust the recipe and taste again right away. Tasting the whole bowl every time you adjust (full-batch gradient descent) would technically be more accurate per check, but you'd run out of dough — and time — long before perfecting the recipe.

$$ \nabla f(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla f_i(\theta) $$

Instead of averaging the gradient over every example in the whole dataset, SGD averages over a small random batch $B$ of examples. It's a noisy estimate of the true gradient — but a vastly cheaper one to compute, so you can take many more steps in the same amount of time.

$$ \mathbb{E}_B\!\left[\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla f_i(\theta)\right] = \nabla f(\theta) $$

This is the formal "unbiasedness" guarantee: if you averaged the mini-batch gradient over every possible random batch you could have drawn, you'd recover the true full-dataset gradient exactly. Any single batch is noisy, but the noise has no systematic direction — it doesn't consistently pull you off-course, it just jitters around the correct path.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

People expect a noisy path to mean a worse final answer. It almost never does, for one key reason: the noise averages out over many steps, since each batch's gradient is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient. The wobble you see is real, but it's wobble around the correct downhill direction, not a random walk in an arbitrary direction.

$$ \mathbb{E}_B\!\left[\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla f_i(\theta)\right] = \nabla f(\theta) $$

This is the formal version of "the noise averages out": if you imagine drawing infinitely many random batches and averaging their gradient estimates, you land exactly on the true full-batch gradient. Any single batch is noisy, but the noise has no systematic bias pulling it in a wrong direction — which is precisely why the jittery red path in the simulator still reliably drifts toward the true minimum.

Reveal the why: how can adding noise ever help, rather than hurt?

Recall Chapter 4's "egg carton" landscape of shallow false valleys. A perfectly smooth, noise-free descent can get permanently trapped the instant it lands in one. SGD's jitter occasionally jostles it just enough to escape a shallow dip, while a true, deep valley is wide and steep enough that the noise can't kick it back out. The very imprecision that seems like a flaw is what gives it an escape hatch plain gradient descent lacks.

Teach it back

In your own words: why can a noisier, cheaper gradient estimate often reach a good answer faster than a perfectly accurate one?

Computing the exact gradient over an entire huge dataset is expensive, so each full-batch step takes a long time. A small random batch gives a noisy but unbiased estimate of that same gradient for a fraction of the cost, letting you take many more steps in the same wall-clock time — and the resulting noise can even help the optimizer hop out of small, shallow local minima.

Worked example — full-batch vs. mini-batch gradient, with real numbers

Suppose a tiny "dataset" has 4 examples, each contributing a per-example gradient: $\nabla f_1=3,\ \nabla f_2=5,\ \nabla f_3=1,\ \nabla f_4=7$.

  1. Full-batch gradient (average over all 4): $\nabla f = \frac{3+5+1+7}{4} = \frac{16}{4} = 4$.
  2. Mini-batch of size 2, randomly drawing examples 1 and 3: $\frac{3+1}{2} = 2$ — noisy: off from the true value of 4 by $-2$.
  3. A different mini-batch, drawing examples 2 and 4: $\frac{5+7}{2} = 6$ — also noisy, this time off by $+2$.
  4. Average the two batch estimates together: $\frac{2+6}{2} = 4$ — exactly the full-batch value, illustrating the unbiasedness formula above: individual batches wobble, but they wobble around the truth.

In a real model, "examples" are training data points and computing all four per-example gradients to get the true average would be the expensive part — a mini-batch sidesteps that cost at the price of exactly this kind of wobble.

Key takeaway

When data is huge, trade per-step accuracy for many more, cheaper steps. Stochastic gradient descent's noise isn't a flaw to tolerate — it's often a feature that helps escape shallow false valleys.

CH 09 EXPERT SYNTHESIS

The Expert Synthesis: One Hill, Every AI Model

Every idea you've learned — the score, the slope, the gradient, the risk of false valleys, the rules of constraints, the art of step size — combines into one machine. That machine is, mechanically, how every modern AI model learns.

What you think

Training a large language model is some fundamentally different, much more mysterious process than the simple hill-rolling you practiced in this course.

What's actually true

Mechanically, it is the exact same loop: define a score (how wrong the model's predictions are), compute the local gradient (in millions or billions of directions at once instead of one or two), and take a step against it. The landscape just has an almost incomprehensibly large number of dimensions — the algorithm itself is unchanged.

Every concept from this course feeding into one loop.

🧠

Putting it together — training an LLM

A language model's "objective function" is how wrong its next-word guesses are across huge amounts of text. Its "θ" isn't one or two numbers — it's billions of adjustable weights, so its landscape has billions of dimensions, far beyond what anyone could visualize. But the loop is unchanged: measure the score, compute the gradient (using a clever bookkeeping trick called backpropagation), take a scaled, momentum-aided step against that gradient using small random batches of text (stochastic gradient descent, from Chapter 8) rather than the whole internet at once, and repeat — millions of times. There is no extra magic beyond what you already practiced on a single hill.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

The word "training" makes people imagine something closer to teaching a student than running an algorithm. Resist that framing: there is no understanding happening step-by-step — there is a number going down, computed the same mechanical way every time, repeated an enormous number of times. The "intelligence" that emerges is a byproduct of scale and clever architecture, not of the optimization loop doing anything conceptually new.

The one piece of new machinery: the chain rule

A neural network isn't one function — it's a chain of functions stacked on top of each other, layer feeding into layer. To get the gradient of the final score with respect to a weight buried deep inside that chain, you need the chain rule from calculus, applied automatically and efficiently. That's the entirety of what backpropagation is.

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_2} \cdot \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial a_1} \cdot \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_1} $$

If a loss $L$ depends on layer-2 output $a_2$, which depends on layer-1 output $a_1$, which depends on weight $w_1$, the chain rule says: multiply the local derivatives along the chain. Backpropagation computes this product layer by layer, working backward from the loss — reusing each layer's partial result instead of recomputing it from scratch for every single weight.

📐 Worked example: backpropagating through a two-layer toy network

Let $a_1 = w_1 x$, $a_2 = w_2 a_1$, and loss $L = (a_2 - y)^2$. Suppose $x=2$, $y=10$, $w_1=1.5$, $w_2=2$.

  1. Forward pass: $a_1 = 1.5 \times 2 = 3$, then $a_2 = 2 \times 3 = 6$, so $L = (6-10)^2 = 16$.
  2. $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_2} = 2(a_2-y) = 2(6-10) = -8$.
  3. $\frac{\partial a_2}{\partial a_1} = w_2 = 2$.
  4. $\frac{\partial a_1}{\partial w_1} = x = 2$.
  5. Chain them together: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = (-8)(2)(2) = -32$.

A gradient descent step with $\alpha=0.01$ would update $w_1 \to 1.5 - 0.01(-32) = 1.82$ — nudging the network toward a lower loss, using nothing but the same "score, slope, step" loop from Chapter 3, applied through one extra layer of bookkeeping.
