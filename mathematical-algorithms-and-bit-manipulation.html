PART ONE

Mathematical Algorithms

A first-principles tour in two parts — Part One: the number theory every competitive programmer eventually needs. Part Two: the bit-level tricks built on top of it. Built the way you'd derive it on a whiteboard, not memorize it from a textbook.

How to use this deck: each idea ends with a question it can't yet answer — the next slide is the answer. Press → or click to begin.

FOUNDATIONS · 01

GCD: shrink two numbers to their shared root

Two numbers can share many divisors. Which single number captures all of them at once — and can we find it without checking every candidate?

Euclid's insight (≈300 BC): gcd(a,b) = gcd(b, a mod b). Repeat until the remainder hits 0 — the last nonzero remainder is the answer.

FOUNDATIONS · 02

That loop shrinks faster than you'd expect

Recap — gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,a mod b) trims the pair every step. How many steps could that possibly take for huge numbers?

Practical uses: reducing fractions to lowest terms, testing coprimality for hash design, and key generation in RSA — all lean on this loop finishing in a handful of steps.

FOUNDATIONS · 03

Extended Euclid: recover the coefficients, not just the number

Recap — Euclid's loop finds gcd(a,b) fast. But it throws away the quotients on the way down. What if we need x, y such that ax + by = gcd(a,b)?

This is Bézout's identity: for any a, b there exist integers x, y with ax + by = gcd(a,b). Extended Euclid recovers them by walking back up the same steps.

FOUNDATIONS · 04

Bézout's coefficients unlock division under a modulus

Recap — extended Euclid gives x,y with ax+by=gcd(a,b). If gcd(a,m)=1, that equation says ax ≡ 1 (mod m). What does that x actually let us do?

That x is a−1 mod m — a modular inverse. It's how Diophantine equations get solved, and it's one of two standard ways to "divide" inside modular arithmetic (the other: Fermat's little theorem, coming up).

FOUNDATIONS · 05

LCM rides on the back of GCD

Recap — gcd and Bézout coefficients are solved. Two events repeat every 12 and 18 days — on what day do they next coincide?

Compute gcd first, then divide before multiplying (a/gcd)×b — multiplying a×b first can overflow even when the final LCM easily fits in a 64-bit integer.

PRIMES & SIEVES · 06

Testing one number for primality

Recap — we can now combine, divide, and synchronize numbers exactly. But is 97 prime? Do we really need to try all 96 possible divisors?

If n has a factor larger than √n, it must pair with one smaller than √n — so trial division only needs to run up to √n. Practical use: quick primality checks inside larger algorithms (crypto key candidates, hashing moduli).

PRIMES & SIEVES · 07

Trial division doesn't scale — and doesn't need to

Recap — O(√n) trial division tests one number. A crypto system needs a 300-digit prime candidate. Even √n is astronomically large — now what?

Real systems use probabilistic tests (Fermat, Miller–Rabin): pick random witnesses and check a modular identity. Wrong-answer probability shrinks below 4−k after k rounds — fast enough for RSA key generation.

PRIMES & SIEVES · 08

Sieve of Eratosthenes: test all numbers at once

Recap — testing one number takes O(√n). What if we need every prime up to 10,000,000 — testing each one individually would take forever. Can we do better in bulk?

Cross out multiples of each prime starting from 2. Every composite gets crossed out by its smallest prime factor. Total cost: O(n log log n) — essentially linear.

PRIMES & SIEVES · 09

The sieve's byproducts are as useful as the primes

Recap — one sweep marks every composite in O(n log log n). While we're marking multiples anyway, can we get more than a yes/no prime flag?

Store the smallest prime factor (SPF) at each crossing instead of just marking it. Then factorizing any number ≤ n becomes a fast repeated lookup — no re-division needed.

PRIMES & SIEVES · 10

Segmented sieve: the range is huge, memory isn't

Recap — the plain sieve needs an array of size n. Now find every prime between 109 and 109+106. Allocating a billion-sized array just isn't possible — what do we do differently?

First sieve small primes up to √(high) normally (cheap). Then sweep only the target window, crossing out multiples of those small primes inside it. Memory drops from O(high) to O(√high + window size).

PRIMES & SIEVES · 11

Bridging bulk sieves and huge ranges

Recap — segmented sieve answers "which primes lie in [L,R]" without O(R) memory. We can now find, factor, and test primes at any scale — but every one of these algorithms so far risks a silent bug: overflow. How do real systems avoid it?

Practical use: range prime-counting queries, prime-gap studies, and precomputation for number-theoretic contest problems where R can reach 1012 or beyond.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 12

A clock face where numbers wrap around

Contest answers are often asked "mod 109+7" — not because the number is arbitrary, but because the true answer is too large to store. What rules survive the wraparound?

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication all pass through the mod safely: (a+b) mod m = ((a mod m)+(b mod m)) mod m, and likewise for ×. Division does not pass through directly — that's the next gap.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 13

Binary exponentiation: skip most of the multiplications

Recap — multiplication survives the mod. But a^b with b around 109 would need a billion multiplications one at a time. Is there a shortcut hiding in binary?

Write the exponent in binary. Square the base at every bit; multiply the running answer in only where a bit is 1. This turns O(b) multiplications into O(log b).

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 14

Every big-exponent formula routes through this trick

Recap — O(log b) modular power via binary exponentiation. We can now exponentiate mod p instantly — but can we compute mod-p division: a ÷ b (mod p)?

Practical uses: RSA's modpow(message, exponent, n) encryption step, and computing large factorials or nCr mod a prime, both of which lean on fast exponentiation internally.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 15

Division mod p, route 1: Fermat's little theorem

Recap — the gap left open earlier: how do you divide under a modulus? If p is prime, is there a formula for a−1 mod p that reuses fast exponentiation?

Fermat's little theorem: if p is prime and gcd(a,p)=1, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Rearranging gives a−1 ≡ ap−2 (mod p) — one call to fast exponentiation.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 16

Division mod p, route 2: when p isn't prime

Recap — Fermat's route needs p prime. What if the modulus isn't prime — does modular inverse still exist, and how do we compute it?

As long as gcd(a,m)=1, extended Euclid's x from earlier is the inverse: ax ≡ 1 (mod m). Practical use: precomputing factorial inverses 1..n in O(n) for repeated nCr mod p queries, instead of O(log p) per query.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 17

Chinese Remainder Theorem: rebuild a number from its shadows

Recap — a single modulus is fully solved: add, multiply, exponentiate, divide. Now suppose you're told x≡2(mod 3) and x≡3(mod 5). What single number below 15 satisfies both?

If the moduli are pairwise coprime, exactly one solution exists mod (m1×m2). Practical use: reconstructing a large value from several small-modulus computations, and RSA-CRT, which speeds up decryption ~4× by working mod p and mod q separately.

MODULAR ARITHMETIC · 18

CRT turns "one hard problem" into "two easy ones"

Recap — CRT rebuilds a unique x from coprime-modulus remainders. We can now compute, divide, and reconstruct numbers under any modulus — what's left before we can start counting arrangements and possibilities?

RSA-CRT: decrypt mod p and mod q separately (smaller exponents, smaller moduli), then combine with CRT — roughly 4× faster than one full-size modular exponentiation.

COUNTING · 19

Counting without listing

Recap — arithmetic under any modulus is fully in hand. How many ways can 3 of 5 friends be chosen for a team — without writing out every group?

Order matters → permutations: nPr = n!/(n−r)!. Order doesn't matter → combinations: nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!). Practical use: probability calculations, counting valid paths/arrangements in DP.

COUNTING · 20

Pascal's triangle: build nCr without factorials

Recap — nCr counts unordered choices via factorials. Factorials of large n overflow fast even under a modulus if computed naively each time — is there a cheaper recurrence?

C(n,r) = C(n−1,r−1) + C(n−1,r) — each entry is the sum of the two above it. Practical use: precomputing all C(n,r) for small n in O(n2), or precomputing factorials + modular inverses once for O(1) queries at large n.

COUNTING · 21

Matrix exponentiation: fast-forward a recurrence

Recap — we can count combinations quickly. Fibonacci(1018) can't be reached by looping one step at a time — not even close. Can binary exponentiation's trick apply to more than plain numbers?

Write the recurrence as a matrix: [F(n+1), F(n)] = M × [F(n), F(n−1)], with M = [[1,1],[1,0]]. Then F(n) needs only Mn, computed with the same binary-exponentiation trick — O(log n) matrix multiplications.

COUNTING · 22

When exact isn't required: probability algorithms

Recap — deterministic exact algorithms cover gcd, primes, mod arithmetic, and counting. Some problems are too slow to solve exactly at scale — when is trading certainty for speed the right call?

Randomized primality tests, reservoir sampling, and Monte Carlo estimation all trade a tiny, controllable error probability for large speedups. Practical use: quick pre-filtering before an exact check, or estimating answers where exact computation is infeasible.

SUMMARY · 23

The full map, traced back to one idea

Every technique in this deck answers a question the previous one couldn't — and nearly all of them lean on the same two primitives underneath.

Everything above reduces to: Euclid's shrinking loop and binary exponentiation's halving trick — applied to numbers, to matrices, and to remainders alike.

PART TWO

From numbers to the switches underneath them

Recap — Part One built an entire toolkit on top of ordinary integers: gcd, primes, modular arithmetic, counting. But every one of those integers is, underneath, just a row of on/off switches. What can we do once we operate on the switches directly?

Part Two: Bit Manipulation — AND / OR / XOR / NOT, shifts, masks, and the tricks built from them.

BIT MANIPULATION

Bit Manipulation

Every trick in this deck comes from one fact: a computer only ever sees rows of on/off switches. Everything else is built from four operators and two shifts.

How to use this deck: each idea ends with a question the current toolkit can't answer — the next slide answers it. Press → or click to begin.

FOUNDATIONS · 01

A number is just a row of switches

We're used to place values in base 10 (hundreds, tens, ones). What are the "place values" when there are only two digits, 0 and 1?

Each position is a power of 2. 13 = 8+4+1 = 23+22+20 — binary 00001101. This one representation is what every bit trick in this deck operates on directly.

CORE OPERATORS · 02

AND keeps only what both switches agree on

Recap — a number is a row of on/off switches. How do we check whether a specific switch is on, without disturbing any of the others?

a & b is 1 only where both bits are 1. Practical use: masking — n & 1 tests if n is odd; n & mask isolates chosen bits (permission checks, extracting fields from packed data).

CORE OPERATORS · 03

OR turns a switch on if either side wants it on

Recap — AND isolates agreement. How do we turn a specific switch ON without touching the others, when we don't know their current state?

a | b is 1 if either bit is 1. Practical use: setting flags — combine permissions with READ | WRITE | EXEC, or turn on a single feature bit with n | (1<<k).

CORE OPERATORS · 04

XOR lights up exactly where two rows disagree

Recap — AND finds agreement, OR finds any support. Is there an operator that detects the DIFFERENCE between two states — and quietly cancels itself out?

a ^ b is 1 only where the bits differ. Two useful facts fall out immediately: a ^ a = 0 and a ^ 0 = a — the engine behind every XOR trick later in this deck.

CORE OPERATORS · 05

NOT flips every switch at once

Recap — AND / OR / XOR combine two rows. What if we only have one row, and we want to build the exact opposite pattern — e.g. "every bit except these"?

~n flips every bit. On signed integers this gives ~n = −n − 1 (two's complement). Practical use: ~mask clears exactly the bits mask would set — used constantly for "clear bit" (coming up next).

SHIFTS · 06

Shifting left multiplies by a power of two

Recap — NOT flips a whole row. Multiplying by 2 in binary should be simple — what does that operation actually look like on the switches?

n << k slides every bit left by k, filling with zeros — equivalent to n × 2k. Watch the sign/overflow: shifting past the integer's bit width silently loses the top bits.

SHIFTS · 07

Shifting right divides — and remembers the sign

Recap — left shift multiplies by 2k. Does right shift just divide by 2k the same clean way — even for negative numbers?

n >> k is floor(n / 2k) for non-negative n. For signed negatives, an arithmetic shift refills with the sign bit (1s) instead of 0s, so the sign is preserved — a logical shift would corrupt it.

BIT MASKS · 08

Shifts build the exact mask you need

Recap — shifts move bits, division/multiplication for free. To touch ONE bit out of 32 without disturbing the rest, we need a stencil with exactly that bit lit. How do we build one on demand?

1 << k makes a mask with only bit k set. (1 << k) − 1 makes a mask of the bottom k bits all set. Practical use: permission flags — READ=1<<0, WRITE=1<<1, EXEC=1<<2, combined with OR.

BIT MASKS · 09

Set, clear, and toggle — one bit, surgically

Recap — 1<<k builds a single-bit stencil. With that stencil in hand, how exactly do we turn bit k ON, OFF, or FLIP it — using only AND / OR / XOR?

Set: n | (1<<k)  ·  Clear: n & ~(1<<k)  ·  Toggle: n ^ (1<<k). Practical use: dirty-bit tracking, visited markers, and flipping individual configuration flags.

BIT TRICKS · 10

Counting the 1-bits without checking every position

Recap — we can set, clear, toggle single bits. To count how many bits are ON, must we test all 32 or 64 positions one by one?

Brian Kernighan's trick: n & (n−1) deletes the lowest set bit. Repeat until n is 0; the number of iterations is the popcount. Cost drops from O(bit-width) to O(number of set bits). Used for Hamming-weight/error-detection codes and counting subset sizes.

BIT TRICKS · 11

Spotting a power of two in one operation

Recap — n & (n−1) strips the lowest set bit. A power of two has exactly ONE bit set — so what happens when we strip that single bit away?

n > 0 && (n & (n−1)) == 0. A power of two loses its only bit and becomes exactly 0; any other number still has a bit left over. Practical use: checking table sizes, memory alignment, and buffer capacities that must stay powers of two.

XOR TRICKS · 12

XOR can swap two values with no third variable

Recap — a^a=0 and a^0=a. Those two facts alone — can they be chained to swap a and b without ever storing either in a temporary slot?

a^=b; b^=a; a^=b; performs a full swap. Honest caveat: on modern hardware a plain temp-variable swap is just as fast and far more readable — this trick's real value is the self-cancelling identity it demonstrates, reused in the next two slides for genuinely useful problems.

XOR TRICKS · 13

Finding the one element that isn't paired

Recap — XOR cancels equal values to 0 and passes lone values through. A list has every number appearing exactly twice, except one. Can we find that one number in a single pass, using no extra memory?

XOR every element together. Every pair cancels to 0, leaving only the unpaired value — O(n) time, O(1) space. This is a classic technical-interview problem and a real building block in some parity/checksum schemes.

BITMASK SUBSETS · 14

A number can represent an entire set

Recap — XOR tricks work element by element. For a set of just 3 items, there are 8 possible subsets. Could a single integer 0..7 stand for each ENTIRE subset at once?

Bit i of a mask means "item i is included." Counting from 0 to 2n−1 enumerates every subset of an n-item set. Practical use: brute-force search over small sets (n ≲ 20) — e.g. trying every combination of items for a knapsack.

BITMASK SUBSETS · 15

Walking only the submasks of a mask

Recap — a mask represents a subset. Sometimes we only need the subsets OF one particular subset (e.g. mask 1011) — not all 2n of them. Can we skip straight to just those?

for(sub=mask; sub; sub=(sub−1)&mask) visits every submask of mask exactly once, largest to smallest, ending at 0. Practical use: sum-over-subsets DP and profile/broken-profile dynamic programming, where total work across all masks is 3n, not 4n.

BITWISE DP · 16

The DP state IS a bitmask

Recap — a mask names a subset in O(1) space. Some DP problems need to track exactly "which items have been used so far" — how do we fold a whole subset into a single, indexable DP dimension?

dp[mask][last] = best cost having visited exactly the cities in mask, ending at city last. Transition: from dp[mask][last], move to any unvisited city j via dp[mask | (1<<j)][j]. This is the classic Held–Karp formulation for the Traveling Salesman Problem.

BITWISE DP · 17

Why 2ⁿ beats n! for exact TSP

Recap — bitmask DP tracks visited-set + current city as one state. Brute force tries every permutation of n cities — n! routes. Does the DP formulation actually beat that in practice?

Held–Karp runs in O(2n · n2) versus brute force's O(n!). Practical use: exact optimal solutions for TSP-like assignment problems up to roughly n=20 — far beyond what permutation brute force could ever reach.

SUMMARY · 18

The full toolkit, traced back to one idea

Every trick in this deck is AND / OR / XOR / NOT / shift, combined and pointed at a specific goal.

Reach for bit tricks when: state fits in a small fixed set (subset DP), you're touching flags/permissions, or you're in a performance-critical loop where a shift beats a multiply. Otherwise, prefer whatever reads clearest — these tools earn their complexity only when the problem asks for it.
