Twelve ideas, seen rather than memorized — the shape data takes, and how we describe that shape.

01 / MEAN

Mean

x̄ = Σxᵢ / n= (2+3+5+7+8) / 5= 25 / 5 = 5
The mean is the point where the data balances — a seesaw with the numbers as weights.

Mean

  • Definition: the arithmetic average — sum of all values divided by how many there are.
  • Population vs. sample: μ = (1/N)Σxᵢ for a whole population, x̄ = (1/n)Σxᵢ for a sample — same formula, different symbol depending on what data you have.
  • Why it balances: the mean is the one value that makes the deviations on either side sum to exactly zero, Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0.
  • Sensitive to outliers: one extreme value can drag the mean far from where most of the data actually sits.
  • Best suited to: roughly symmetric, interval/ratio-scale data without heavy skew.
  • Where the formula comes from: ask "what single number c best represents this data?" and define "best" as minimizing total squared error, Σ(xᵢ−c)². Take the derivative with respect to c and set it to zero — the answer that falls out is exactly the mean. It isn't picked by convention; it's the unique least-squares answer.
  • Real-world use: GPA, average order value in e-commerce dashboards, expected return of an investment in finance, average sensor reading in engineering.
  • Practical caution: "average revenue per customer" can hide whether it's driven by a few big spenders or spread evenly — always pair the mean with a spread measure (variance/std dev) or the median.
02 / MEDIAN

Median

sorted: 2, 4, 5, 7, 9n=5 (odd) → median = x₍₃₎ = 5
Line the values up in order. The median is whoever is standing in the middle — unbothered by how far the extremes wander.

Median

  • Definition: the middle value of sorted data — the 50th percentile.
  • Formula: odd n → x₍₍n+1₎/2₎; even n → average of the two middle values, (x₍n/2₎ + x₍n/2+1₎)/2.
  • Robust: moving the largest value in a dataset arbitrarily far away doesn't move the median at all — unlike the mean.
  • Reading skew: mean > median usually signals a right-skewed distribution (long tail toward high values); mean < median signals left skew.
  • Best suited to: skewed data or data with outliers — income, house prices, response times.
  • Where the formula comes from: instead of minimizing squared error like the mean, the median minimizes total absolute error, Σ|xᵢ−c|. Absolute value grows linearly with distance instead of quadratically — that's the precise mathematical reason a huge outlier barely moves it.
  • Real-world use: the U.S. Census reports median household income specifically to avoid a handful of billionaires skewing the picture; median home price listings; median page-load time.
  • Practical caution: if a company reports "average salary" and it includes a few highly-paid executives, ask for the median instead — it tells you what a typical employee actually earns.
03 / MODE

Mode

freq(2)=1, freq(3)=3, freq(5)=2, freq(7)=1mode = argmax freq(v) = 3
Stack repeats on top of each other. The mode is simply the tallest pile — the value that shows up most.

Mode

  • Definition: the most frequently occurring value (or values) in the data.
  • Only central-tendency measure for nominal data — you can't average "red, blue, blue, green," but you can find its mode.
  • Shape: a distribution can be unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal; multiple peaks often hint at mixed subpopulations hiding in one dataset.
  • For continuous data, "mode" usually refers to the peak of a fitted density curve rather than one exactly-repeated number.
  • Least used of the three central tendencies for numeric data, but essential for categorical summaries.
  • Where it comes from: unlike mean/median, mode isn't the solution to a minimization problem — it directly answers "which value shows up most," which is exactly why it works on categories with no numeric meaning at all.
  • Real-world use: the most common shoe size a retailer stocks, the most frequently selected subscription plan, the most common word in a body of text (a core step in NLP).
  • Practical caution: a retailer plans inventory around the mode shoe size, not the mean — you can't stock "size 8.3." Whenever the "average" of your data wouldn't be a valid value, look for the mode instead.
04 / VARIANCE

Variance

σ² = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²= (9+4+0+4+9) / 5= 26 / 5 = 5.2
Measure each point's distance from the mean, then square it — far points get punished disproportionately. Variance is the average of those squares.

Variance

  • Definition: the average squared deviation from the mean — one number summarizing spread.
  • Population: σ² = (1/N)Σ(xᵢ−μ)². Sample: s² = (1/(n−1))Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² — the n−1 is Bessel's correction, needed to keep the sample variance an unbiased estimate of the population variance.
  • Why square instead of using |xᵢ−x̄|? Squaring is differentiable everywhere (unlike absolute value) and penalizes large deviations more — it's the choice that makes the mean the optimal "center."
  • Units are squared (e.g. dollars²), which is awkward to interpret — that's the whole motivation for standard deviation.
  • Foundation for nearly everything downstream: std dev, covariance, correlation, confidence intervals, and ANOVA are all built on this idea.
  • Why squaring specifically: squared deviations are smooth (differentiable everywhere), which is what let calculus derive the mean as the optimal center in the first place. They also have a rare, powerful property — for independent variables, variances simply add: Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y). Absolute deviations don't have that property, which is why most of statistics builds on squared error, not absolute error.
  • Why n−1, concretely: x̄ is computed from the same sample, and by construction it's the value that minimizes Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² for that particular sample — so this sum is systematically a little smaller than if you'd used the true (unknown) μ. Dividing by n−1 instead of n corrects exactly for that shrinkage, on average, across many samples.
  • Real-world use: variance of investment returns = financial risk; variance in manufacturing measurements = quality-control tolerances; variance of a conversion rate directly determines how large an A/B test sample needs to be.
05 / STANDARD DEVIATION

Standard Deviation

σ = √σ² = √5.2 ≈ 2.28P(μ−1σ < X < μ+1σ) ≈ 68%P(μ−2σ < X < μ+2σ) ≈ 95%
Undo the squaring with a square root, and variance shrinks back to the original units — a ruler you can lay against the data.

Standard Deviation

  • Definition: σ = √σ² (or s = √s² for a sample) — variance brought back into the original units.
  • Empirical rule (68-95-99.7): holds for roughly bell-shaped (normal) distributions only — it is not guaranteed for skewed or heavy-tailed data.
  • Chebyshev's inequality is the distribution-free fallback: for any distribution, at least 1 − 1/k² of the data lies within k standard deviations of the mean.
  • A small σ means the data huddles close to the mean; a large σ means it's spread wide — σ is the "typical distance" from center.
  • The coefficient of variation (σ/mean) is a handy way to compare spread across datasets with different units or scales.
  • Where the square root comes from: it undoes the squaring in variance for one reason only — to get back to the original, interpretable units. "Spread in dollars," not "spread in dollars²."
  • Real-world use: stock volatility is literally quoted as the standard deviation of returns; Six Sigma quality control is named after keeping defects beyond ±6σ from target; standardized tests (SAT, GRE) scale scores using σ.
  • Practical caution: "this drug lowers blood pressure by 10 ± 2 mmHg" — that ± 2 is a standard deviation (or standard error), and it's what tells you how much to trust the "10."
06 / COVARIANCE

Covariance

cov(X,Y) = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)same-sign pair → +areaopposite-sign pair → −area
For every point, multiply its distance from mean-X by its distance from mean-Y. Same-direction pairs add positive area; opposite pairs add negative area.

Covariance

  • Definition: cov(X,Y) = (1/n)Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ) — the average product of paired deviations.
  • Sign tells direction: positive → X and Y tend to rise together; negative → one rises as the other falls; near zero → no consistent linear pattern.
  • cov(X,X) = Var(X) — variance is just the covariance of a variable with itself.
  • The catch: covariance's magnitude depends on the units of X and Y, so a covariance of "12" is meaningless without context — you can't compare it across different variable pairs.
  • That scaling problem is exactly what correlation, next, is built to fix.
  • Where the formula comes from: if X and Y are both above their means at once (or both below), the product of their deviations is positive; if one is above while the other is below, it's negative. Summing these products literally tallies "how often, and how strongly, do they move together."
  • Real-world use: the mathematical basis of portfolio diversification (Markowitz theory) — combining assets with negative covariance lowers overall portfolio risk.
  • Practical caution: covariance tells you the direction of a relationship (ad spend up → sales up), but not how strong it is per unit — for that you need correlation or regression.
07 / CORRELATION

Correlation

r = cov(X,Y) / (σx · σy)−1 ≤ r ≤ 1
Correlation is covariance rescaled to always land between −1 and +1 — a unit-free score for how tightly points hug a line.

Correlation

  • Definition (Pearson's r): r = cov(X,Y) / (σx · σy) — covariance divided by both standard deviations, always between −1 and +1.
  • r = ±1 → perfect straight-line relationship. r = 0 → no linear relationship — there could still be a strong curved one r won't detect.
  • Correlation ≠ causation: two variables can move together because of a shared underlying cause, coincidence, or even reverse causation.
  • r², the "coefficient of determination," is the share of one variable's variance that's explained by a linear fit with the other.
  • Like the mean, r is sensitive to outliers — a single extreme point can swing it substantially.
  • Where the bound of ±1 comes from: dividing by σx·σy isn't arbitrary — the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality guarantees |cov(X,Y)| ≤ σx·σy always, for any data. That's the actual mathematical proof that r can never exceed 1 in magnitude.
  • Real-world use: correlating ad spend with revenue, screen time with grades, height with weight; a standard step in machine learning feature selection, to drop redundant, highly-correlated inputs.
  • Practical caution: two variables can correlate strongly by pure chance in a small sample ("spurious correlation") — always sanity-check with domain knowledge, and a controlled experiment if you need to claim causation.
08 / PERCENTILES

Percentiles

rank = ⌈ (p/100) × n ⌉Pₖ = value at that rank, sorted
Sort the data, then sweep from left to right. The k-th percentile is where k% of the data is already behind you.

Percentiles

  • Definition: the k-th percentile is the value below which k% of the (sorted) data falls.
  • Quartiles are special percentiles: Q1 = P25, Q2 = P50 (the median), Q3 = P75.
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1 (interquartile range) is a robust spread measure, and the usual basis for flagging outliers — commonly anything beyond 1.5×IQR past Q1 or Q3.
  • Several valid interpolation methods exist (nearest-rank, linear, etc.) — different software can report slightly different percentile values for identical data.
  • Used for: standardized test scores, growth charts, latency/performance benchmarking (e.g. "p99 latency").
  • Why rank-based, not value-based: percentiles ignore the actual gaps between values and only care about order — that's exactly what makes them immune to a single huge outlier, and why a percentile rank survives a transform like converting to log-scale, while a raw value or z-score doesn't.
  • Real-world use: pediatric growth charts (height/weight-for-age percentiles), test-score percentiles (SAT, GRE), and the software-engineering standard of reporting "p99 latency" as a reliability target.
  • Practical caution: p99 latency matters more than average latency for user experience — the average can look great while 1% of users are having a genuinely terrible, slow experience.
09 / CONFIDENCE INTERVAL

Confidence Interval

CI = x̄ ± z* · (σ / √n)95% CI → z* ≈ 1.96
One interval either does or doesn't contain the truth. But repeat the sampling many times, and about 95% of the intervals will catch it.

Confidence Interval

  • Definition: a range, built from sample data, likely to contain the true population parameter.
  • Formula: CI = x̄ ± z*·(σ/√n) — or with a t-distribution instead of z when σ is unknown and n is small.
  • "95% confidence" describes the procedure, not one specific interval: over many repeated samples, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true value.
  • Common misconception: "there's a 95% probability the true mean is in this interval" — the true mean is fixed; the randomness is in which sample (and interval) you happened to draw.
  • Wider interval ↔ more confidence or more variability; narrower interval ↔ larger sample size.
  • Where σ/√n comes from: it's the standard error of the mean, and it follows directly from the Central Limit Theorem — averaging n independent draws shrinks the variance by a factor of n (Var(x̄) = σ²/n), so the spread of the sampling distribution shrinks by √n. More data narrows the interval, but with diminishing returns: you need 4× the data to halve the width.
  • Where z* ≈ 1.96 comes from: it's simply the point on the standard normal curve where 95% of the area falls within ±z* of the center — read directly off the normal distribution.
  • Real-world use: political polling ("42% ± 3%, 19 times out of 20"), clinical-trial effect sizes, manufacturing tolerance ranges, A/B test result reporting.
10 / HYPOTHESIS TESTING

Hypothesis Testing

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)reject H₀ if |z| > z_crit
Assume nothing is going on (H₀). If the data lands somewhere that assumption makes unlikely, we start to doubt it.

Hypothesis Testing

  • H₀ (null): the default "nothing interesting is happening" claim. H₁ (alternative): what you'd conclude instead if you reject H₀.
  • A test statistic (z, t, F, χ², ...) measures how far the observed data is from what H₀ predicts, in standardized units.
  • Significance level α (commonly 0.05) is the risk of a false positive you're willing to accept — chosen before seeing the data.
  • Type I error = rejecting a true H₀ (false alarm). Type II error = failing to reject a false H₀ (missed effect). There's a trade-off between them.
  • Failing to reject H₀ is not proof H₀ is true — it just means the data didn't give strong enough evidence against it.
  • Why standardize: converting to z = (x̄−μ₀)/(σ/√n) expresses "how many standard errors away from the null's prediction is my data," in a single number that can be compared against a universal reference — the standard normal table — regardless of the original units.
  • Real-world use: drug trials (does this treatment outperform placebo?), manufacturing (has this process's average defect rate changed?), UX A/B tests (does a new button color change click-through rate?).
  • Practical caution: the test gives a binary decision (reject / fail to reject) — it deliberately says nothing about the size of the effect. Always pair it with an effect size and a confidence interval.
11 / P-VALUE

p-value

p = P(Z ≥ z_obs | H₀ true)reject H₀ if p < α
The p-value is the shaded tail area beyond what we observed — the odds of seeing something this extreme if H₀ were actually true.

p-value

  • Definition: p = P(data this extreme or more | H₀ true) — how surprising the data would be if the null hypothesis were correct.
  • Small p-value → the observed data would be unusual under H₀ → evidence against H₀.
  • A p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true, and it's NOT the probability the result happened "by chance" — these are two of the most common misreadings.
  • Statistical significance ≠ practical significance: with a large enough sample, even a tiny, real-world-meaningless effect can produce p < 0.05.
  • Thresholds like α = 0.05 or 0.01 are conventions, not laws of nature — the "right" threshold depends on the cost of being wrong.
  • Why "or more extreme," not just "this exact value": for continuous data, the chance of any single exact value is essentially zero — so the p-value instead sums up the probability of this result and anything even less consistent with H₀. That's what makes it a meaningful measure of how far into the unlikely tail the data landed.
  • Real-world use: FDA drug-approval processes require pre-registered significance thresholds; scientific journals have historically used p < 0.05 as an informal publication bar.
  • Practical caution: that publication-bar convention is now widely criticized for encouraging "p-hacking." The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement explicitly warns against using a p-value alone to make a decision — always look at effect size and confidence intervals together.
12 / ANOVA

ANOVA

F = MS_between / MS_withinMS = SS / degrees of freedom
Compare the spread between group means to the ordinary noise within each group. A big ratio means the groups really differ.

ANOVA

  • Definition: Analysis of Variance — tests whether three or more group means are likely all equal, in a single test.
  • F-statistic: F = MS_between / MS_within, where each mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.
  • Why not just run many t-tests? Each individual test carries some false-positive risk; testing many pairs inflates the overall chance of a false positive (the "multiple comparisons" problem). One F-test controls this.
  • A significant F says some group differs — it doesn't say which. Post-hoc tests (e.g. Tukey's HSD) are needed to pinpoint that.
  • Assumes: roughly normal, independent groups with similar variances across groups (homogeneity of variance).
  • Why a ratio of variances: if the group means truly differ, the "between-group" variance (how spread out the group means are) should be large relative to the "within-group" variance (ordinary noise inside each group). The F-ratio is precisely engineered to compare that signal to that noise.
  • Real-world use: comparing crop yields across different fertilizers, test scores across different teaching methods, or conversion rates across more than two website designs (multi-variant A/B/C testing).
  • Practical caution: ANOVA only answers "is there a difference somewhere among these groups" — you'll almost always want a follow-up post-hoc test to say specifically which pair differs.
