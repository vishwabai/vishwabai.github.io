Probability Definitions

Probability turns "how likely" into a number between 0 and 1 — the fraction of a sample space that an event covers.

Sample space S is the set of every possible outcome of an experiment — all six die faces, every card in a deck, every token in a vocabulary. An event A is just a subset of S — the outcomes you actually care about ("rolled an even number", "drew a heart").

Classical Probability
P(A) = \dfrac{n(A)}{n(S)} = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total outcomes in } S}
Axioms
0 \le P(A) \le 1 \qquad P(S) = 1 \qquad P(\varnothing) = 0

This is the frequentist picture of probability: roll a die enough times and the observed fraction of any face converges to its true probability, 1/6. Roll a few times — then roll a thousand.

Independent Events

When events truly don't affect each other, their joint probability is just the product. Multiply enough of them and the probability of any one exact outcome collapses fast.

Multiplication rule for independent events
P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A_1)\cdot P(A_2)\cdots P(A_n)

Joint & Marginal Distributions

Two random variables rarely live alone — the real object is their joint distribution. Each variable's own distribution is just that joint table summed down to one axis.

A joint distribution is a table over every combination of outcomes for two variables at once — weather × commute time, token × position, predicted class × true class. Summing a row or column gives you that variable's marginal distribution — what's left once you stop tracking the other variable.

Marginalization
P(X{=}x) = \sum_{y} P(X{=}x, Y{=}y)

Naive Bayes Classifier

A spam filter doesn't understand language — it runs Bayes' theorem on word counts, pretending every word is independent given the label. That lie is what makes it fast, and it works surprisingly well anyway.

"Naive" doesn't mean bad — it names a known-false simplifying assumption: that each word's evidence is independent of the others, given the class. Combine that assumption with Bayes' theorem and you get a classifier that needs nothing more than word frequency counts.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Training a neural net with cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood estimation — choosing the parameter that makes the data you actually saw look as probable as possible.

Likelihood is a function of the parameter, with data held fixed — backwards from probability, which is a function of the data with the parameter fixed. You flip a coin 10 times and see 7 heads; drag θ and watch how probable that exact outcome would have been under each guess.

MAP Estimation & Priors

L2 regularization isn't an engineering hack — it's MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior pulling weights toward zero. Regularization is Bayesian probability, dressed in engineering language.

Same coin, same 7 heads in 10 flips — but now bring a prior belief that fair coins are far more common than biased ones. As data grows, MAP converges to the plain MLE; priors matter most exactly when data is scarce, which is precisely when regularization matters most in practice.

Entropy, Cross-Entropy & KL Divergence

"Cross-entropy loss" isn't an arbitrary name — it's a real measure, in bits, of how surprised your model is by the true answer.

Entropy is the inherent uncertainty in a true distribution P. Cross-entropy is the cost of describing P's outcomes using Q's code instead. KL divergence is the extra cost of that mismatch — cross-entropy minus entropy — and it's always ≥ 0, but not symmetric: KL(P‖Q) ≠ KL(Q‖P).

The three quantities
H(P)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i \qquad H(P,Q)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 q_i \qquad KL(P\Vert Q)=H(P,Q)-H(P)

Markov Chains & Language Models

The simplest language model is a Markov chain you could build by hand: a table of "given this word, what's next" probabilities. Bigram models, PageRank, and weather forecasting all run on the same idea.

The Markov property says the future depends only on the present, not the full history. "Memoryless" doesn't mean the past doesn't matter — it means the past only matters through the current state, which can itself encode a lot of compressed history.

The Markov property
P(X_{t+1}\mid X_t, X_{t-1},\dots,X_0) = P(X_{t+1}\mid X_t)
