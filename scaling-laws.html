the opening puzzle
You added 10× more servers.
Why is it barely faster?
BEFORE — 1 server
120 ms response time
AFTER — 10 servers
108 ms response time
Ten times the hardware bought you a 10% speedup, not a 10× speedup. That's not a bug in your code — it's a law. Several of them, actually. Every system you'll ever scale obeys a small set of rules like this one, and almost nobody is taught them directly. This deck is that missing lesson.

Ch. 1 — Speeding up one job · Amdahl's Law
Some parts of the job just won't parallelize
Picture cooking a big dinner. Chopping vegetables — you can hand out knives to five friends and it's 5× faster. But preheating the oven? Doesn't matter how many friends you have, it still takes 12 minutes. One part scales with helpers. One part doesn't.
serial part parallel part
The rose block (serial, 20% of the work) refuses to shrink no matter how many workers join the amber block.
Ch. 1 · Amdahl's Law — the formula
There is a hard ceiling on your speedup
S(n) = 1 / ( (1−P) + P/n )
P = fraction of the job that's parallelizable. n = number of workers. As n → ∞, the P/n term vanishes and speedup caps out at exactly 1 / (1−P) — the serial fraction is your ceiling, full stop.
If 95% of your code parallelizes perfectly, the absolute best speedup you can ever reach — with infinite servers — is 20×. Not 1000×. Not "the cloud." Twenty.
Practical use: before buying more machines, ask "what % of this workload is genuinely parallel?" — that number is your budget.
RECAP
Amdahl's Law: if the job size is fixed, the unparallelizable slice caps your speedup forever.

→Gustafson's Law
Ch. 1 · Gustafson's Law
Scale the job, not just the speed
Amdahl asks: "same job, more workers — how much faster?" Gustafson flips it: "same time budget, more workers — how much more work can we finish?" A weather-simulation team doesn't run yesterday's forecast 1000× faster — they run a 1000× more detailed forecast in the same overnight window. That's real supercomputing.
S(n) = (1−P) + P·n
Notice: n is no longer trapped inside a fraction — speedup grows linearly with workers, because the serial part shrinks as a percentage of an ever-bigger total job.
Practical use: Amdahl decides "should I parallelize this specific task?" — Gustafson decides "should I scale out for a bigger dataset/model/simulation?"
RECAP
Amdahl and Gustafson: two ways to think about parallel speed on a single system.
GAP
Neither one tells us something a real production system needs every day: how many requests are sitting inside my system right now — and how big should my thread pool even be?

→Little's Law
Ch. 2 — Measuring flow · Little's Law
A coffee shop tells you everything about queues
Customers arrive at 2 per minute. Each one spends 5 minutes in the shop (ordering + waiting + drinking). How many people are in the shop on average, at any random moment you peek in?
2 people/min × 5 min = 10 people, on average, inside the shop at once.
No queueing theory, no simulation — just multiplication. That's the entire trick of Little's Law, and it works for any stable system: web requests, hospital beds, warehouse pallets.
L = λ · W — L: number in system, λ: arrival rate, W: average time in system.
Ch. 2 · Little's Law — sizing a real system
Now size a connection pool with it
L = λ · W
  • λYour API takes 200 requests/second (arrival rate).
  • WEach request holds a DB connection for 50 ms on average (time in system).
  • L200 × 0.05 = 10 concurrent connections, on average, needed.
If you size your pool to 10, you're covering only the average — a burst of slow queries will queue instantly. Real systems size to the average plus headroom for variance. Little's Law gives you the floor, not the ceiling — but the floor is exactly the number most people guess wrong.
Also explains why latency spikes cause cascading outages: rising W (slower responses) mechanically raises L (more requests piling up) — even if arrival rate λ never changed.
Practical use: capacity planning for thread pools, DB connections, checkout queues, hospital ICU beds — anywhere "things flow through a system."
RECAP
Little's Law tells us how much work is in flight inside one system.
GAP
But once that work is spread across many machines talking over a network that can simply stop working mid-conversation — what fundamentally breaks, and what are we forced to give up?

→CAP Theorem
Ch. 3 — Distributed trade-offs · CAP Theorem
Two ATMs, one network cable, one cut
Two ATMs share a balance of $500. Someone withdraws $400 from ATM A at the exact moment the network link between the ATMs is cut.
ATM B has two choices, and only two:
  • CRefuse withdrawals until the link is back (stay Consistent, sacrifice Availability).
  • AAllow withdrawals anyway, risk letting someone take more than exists (stay Available, sacrifice Consistency).
This choice — C or A, during a partition — is CAP Theorem. It's not a design flaw. It's forced.
Ch. 3 · CAP Theorem — the formal statement
Pick 2 of 3 is a myth — pick 2 of 3 only during a partition
The common phrase "you can only have 2 of Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance" is misleading. In a real distributed system, network partitions will happen — so P isn't really optional. The actual choice only appears while a partition is happening: go CP or go AP.
CP example: ZooKeeper, etcd — refuse to answer rather than risk a wrong answer.

AP example: Cassandra, DynamoDB (default mode) — always answer, reconcile conflicts later.
Practical use: when picking a database, ask "what does it do when a node can't reach the others?" — that answer tells you if it's CP or AP.
RECAP
CAP tells us the choice we're forced into during a network partition.
GAP
Partitions are actually rare — most of the time the network is fine. So what trade-off are we making the other 99.9% of the time, when nothing is broken at all?
FILL
→PACELC
Ch. 3 · PACELC — CAP's quieter, more useful sibling
Even with no partition, you're still trading something
if Partition: choose A or C  —  Else: choose Latency or Consistency
Replicating data to 5 regions before confirming a write gives you strong consistency — but it costs latency, because you're waiting on the slowest replica. Confirming immediately and replicating in the background gives you speed — but a reader in another region might briefly see stale data.
DynamoDB / Cassandra → PA/EL: available under partition, favors low latency normally.
Traditional relational DB (single-region, sync replicas) → PC/EC: consistent under partition, favors correctness over speed normally.
Practical use: when a database markets itself as "highly available," ask what it does to latency on the happy path — PACELC is the honest question CAP forgets to ask.
RECAP
CAP + PACELC cover the technical trade-offs a distributed system forces on us.

→Conway's Law
Ch. 4 — Human & network truths · Conway's Law
Your org chart is secretly your architecture diagram
Organizations end up designing systems that copy their own communication structure. Three teams that barely talk to each other will — almost inevitably — produce three services with a thin, awkward API between them, no matter what the architecture doc says should happen.
Not a metaphor — it's a documented, repeated pattern across real engineering orgs.
Ch. 4 · Conway's Law — using it on purpose
The Inverse Conway Maneuver
If team structure leaks into system structure, you can run the trick backwards: decide the architecture you want first, then reorganize the teams to match it — and the architecture tends to follow on its own.
Want independently-deployable microservices? Build small, cross-functional teams that each own one service end-to-end — not a "backend team" and a "frontend team" that must coordinate on every change.
This is why "just split the monolith into microservices" so often fails when the org keeps one giant team meeting for every decision — the communication structure never changed, so the code quietly grows back into one tangled thing regardless of the folder layout.
Practical use: before a re-architecture, redesign team boundaries first — the code structure is a lagging indicator of the team structure.
RECAP
Conway's Law: system structure mirrors the communication structure of the humans building it.
GAP
Speaking of communication — can two independent machines ever be 100% certain they agree, when the only way to talk is a network that might silently eat a message?

→Two Generals Problem
Ch. 4 · The Two Generals Problem
Two armies, one messenger, no certainty
Two generals camp on hills facing a city. They'll only win if they attack at the exact same time. Their only communication is a messenger who can be captured. General A sends "attack at dawn." He needs an acknowledgment to know it arrived — but that ack could also get captured. So he'd need an ack for the ack. Forever.
No finite number of messages can give both generals perfect, shared certainty. This is proven — not an engineering limitation, a mathematical one.
This is why "just add a confirmation message" never fully fixes distributed coordination.
Ch. 4 · Two Generals — what we actually do about it
We don't solve it. We make "wrong" cheap enough.
  • 1TCP handshake doesn't solve it — it just makes the probability of silent disagreement small enough to live with, using timeouts and retries.
  • 2Two-Phase Commit (2PC) gets closer for transactions, but can still block forever if the coordinator dies mid-vote.
  • 3Paxos / Raft accept imperfect certainty and instead guarantee progress despite failures, using majority votes instead of universal agreement.
The mindset shift this law forces: stop designing for "the network will confirm delivery," and start designing for idempotent retries — an operation you can safely repeat if you're not sure it landed the first time, like a payment API that dedupes on a request ID instead of trusting a single "success" response.
Practical use: whenever you see "at-least-once delivery" or "idempotency key" in an API — that's an engineer designing around the Two Generals Problem.
RECAP
Two Generals: perfect agreement over a network is provably impossible.
GAP
If certainty is impossible, engineers must be making quiet, wrong assumptions about the network anyway, every day, without realizing it. What are they?

→8 Fallacies of Distributed Computing
Ch. 4 · Eight Fallacies of Distributed Computing
Eight things every new distributed-systems engineer assumes — wrongly
1. The network is reliable 2. Latency is zero 3. Bandwidth is infinite 4. The network is secure 5. Topology doesn't change 6. There is one administrator 7. Transport cost is zero 8. The network is homogeneous
Originally listed by engineers at Sun Microsystems in the 1990s — every one of these is still quietly assumed by developers today, especially anyone who first learned to code on one machine, where all eight of these happen to be true by accident.
The next three slides zoom into the ones that cause the most real production incidents.
Ch. 4 · Fallacies 1 & 2
"The network is reliable" and "latency is zero"
Fallacy 1
A function call across a network can silently fail halfway — the request left, but you never learn if it arrived. A local function call can't fail that way; a remote one always can.
Fallacy 2
Calling a service in another region "just like calling a local function" ignores that light itself takes ~40ms to cross an ocean — no amount of optimization removes that floor.
Practical use: every remote call needs a timeout and a retry policy — treating it like a local call is where "it worked on my laptop" outages come from.
Ch. 4 · Fallacies 3, 4 & 5
"Bandwidth is infinite," "it's secure," "it never changes"
Fallacy 3 — bandwidth
Shipping full objects between microservices "because JSON is easy" quietly saturates a link that seemed unlimited in dev with 3 test records.
Fallacy 4 — security
Internal traffic isn't automatically safe. Zero-trust architecture exists precisely because "it's inside our network" was never actually a security boundary.
Fallacy 5 — topology
Hardcoding an IP address instead of using service discovery breaks the moment autoscaling adds or removes a node — which is constantly, in the cloud.
Practical use: load-test with production-sized payloads, authenticate service-to-service, and never hardcode addresses.
Ch. 4 · Fallacies 6, 7 & 8
"One admin," "transport is free," "the network is uniform"
Fallacy 6 — one admin
A multi-cloud or multi-team system has multiple owners with different change windows — the "who approved this firewall rule" outage.
Fallacy 7 — transport cost
Serializing, encrypting, and sending data costs real CPU and real money — cross-region egress bills are this fallacy showing up on an invoice.
Fallacy 8 — homogeneous
Not every hop is the same fiber-optic backbone — a mobile client on 3G is part of "the network" too, and your timeout budget has to survive it.
All eight fallacies share one root cause: designing as if the network were a single reliable machine, when it's really many unreliable ones stitched together.
RECAP
The 8 Fallacies: false assumptions engineers make about the network itself.

→Power Law & Zipf's Distribution
Ch. 5 — Shape of real-world data · Power Law / Zipf
A tiny few items get almost all the traffic
Count how often each word appears in any large book. The word "the" appears roughly twice as often as the 2nd most common word, three times as often as the 3rd, and so on. This isn't a coincidence of English — it shows up in city populations, website traffic, and cache hits alike.
frequency(rank k) ∝ 1 / k^s
Practical use: caching. The top handful of keys explain most of your hit rate — cache those aggressively, and don't bother caching the long tail.
Ch. 5 · Power Law — the sharding trap
The "hot key" problem is Zipf in disguise
You shard user data across 100 database partitions to spread load evenly. Looks perfectly balanced on paper — 1% of traffic per shard. Then one celebrity account goes viral and its shard alone takes 40% of all traffic, while 99 shards sit idle.
Sharding assumes uniform distribution. Real usage follows a power law. That mismatch is exactly what creates hot partitions, CDN cache misses concentrated on a few viral assets, and "one tenant broke the platform for everyone" incidents.
Mitigations: key-splitting a hot key across multiple physical partitions, request coalescing (merge duplicate concurrent reads into one), and dedicated caching tiers in front of the top 1% of keys instead of relying on uniform sharding alone.
Practical use: whenever you design for "average load per shard," also ask "what does the peak look like for the single hottest item?"
RECAP
Zipf's Law: a few items dominate frequency — traffic, popularity, usage.
GAP
Is there a simpler, everyday version of this same imbalance — one about effort and results instead of frequency, that applies to your own work?

→The Pareto Principle
Ch. 5 · The Pareto Principle
80% of the outcome comes from 20% of the input
First observed in land ownership, then rediscovered everywhere: roughly 20% of your codebase causes 80% of your production incidents. Roughly 20% of your API endpoints handle 80% of your traffic. It's the same imbalance as Zipf — just used as a rough planning heuristic instead of a precise formula.
Practical use: when triaging tech debt with limited time, find the 20% causing 80% of the pain — that's usually your highest-leverage fix, not the newest code.

    Before you design the next system
    Ask these, in order
    • 1What % of this workload is truly parallel? (Amdahl's ceiling)
    • 2Am I speeding up the same job, or scaling to a bigger one? (Gustafson)
    • 3What's my arrival rate and time-in-system? (Little's Law sizing)
    • 4CP or AP when a partition hits? (CAP)
    • 5Latency or consistency on the happy path? (PACELC)
    • 6Does my team structure match the architecture I want? (Conway)
    • 7Are my remote calls idempotent and retry-safe? (Two Generals)
    • 8Which of the 8 fallacies am I quietly assuming right now?
    • 9What's my single hottest key or endpoint? (Zipf)
    • 10What's the 20% causing 80% of my pain? (Pareto)
    That's the whole toolkit. Nothing here needs memorizing — it needs recognizing, the next time your system surprises you.
