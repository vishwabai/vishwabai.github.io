Foundations
The one question geometry keeps asking: which way do we turn?
Walking A→B→C, do we bend left, bend right, or stay straight? That single fact powers hulls, polygons, and intersections.
Foundations
The cross product turns "which way" into a number
u × v = uₓvᵧ − uᵧvₓ. Positive → v is left of u (CCW). Negative → right (CW). Zero → collinear.
Foundations
RECAP → GAP: cross tells us the turn. What tells us "how aligned"?
u·v = uₓvₓ + uᵧvᵧ = |u||v|cosθ. It measures alignment: v's shadow cast onto u's direction.

Hull & Sorting
Convex hull: the shape a rubber band snaps into
Convex = every segment between two hull points stays inside it. No dent ever points inward.
Hull — Graham Scan (1/3)
Step 1 — anchor on the lowest point
This pivot is guaranteed to sit on the hull — nothing can be "below" it, so it anchors our sweep.
Hull — Graham Scan (2/3)
Step 2 — sort every other point by angle from the pivot
A radar sweep from the pivot, left to right, gives the exact order the hull will visit points in.
Hull — Graham Scan (3/3)
Step 3 — walk the order, pop on a right turn
Keep a stack. For each new point, while the last turn is clockwise, pop — then push. O(n log n) total, driven entirely by the orientation test.
Hull — Alternative
Monotone chain: sort by x, build two chains
Andrew's algorithm (2001): sort points by x, sweep left→right for the lower chain, right→left for the upper — same pop-on-right-turn rule, no angle sort needed.
Bridge
RECAP → GAP: hull finds the outer boundary. But which two points sit closest together?
FILL: checking every pair is O(n²) — divide and conquer brings it to O(n log n).
Closest Pair (1/2)
Baseline: check every pair — O(n²)
n points ⇒ n(n−1)/2 distance checks. Fine for small n, painful at scale — 1 million points is half a trillion pairs.
Closest Pair (2/2)
Split, conquer, then check only the seam
Split by x, solve each half recursively, then only re-check points within δ of the midline — provably at most a handful per point. Total: O(n log n).
Measurement
The shoelace formula: area from coordinates alone
Cross each vertex with the next, sum the signed triangles from the origin — the outer parts cancel and only the polygon's area survives.
Queries
Inside or outside? Fire a ray and count crossings
Odd number of crossings means P is inside; even means outside. Careful edge cases: rays through a vertex must count consistently.
Bridge
RECAP → GAP: we can test one point, one polygon. What about thousands of segments, all at once?
FILL: sweep a line across the plane, and only ever compare neighbors that are actually adjacent right now.
Sweep Line
Turn a 2D problem into a 1D problem, frame by frame
An imaginary vertical line glides left→right; a status structure tracks which segments it currently crosses, ordered top to bottom — intersections can only happen between neighbors that swap order.
Spatial Structures
Voronoi: color the plane by "nearest seed"
Every region is the set of points closer to its seed than any other. The boundary between two regions is always the perpendicular bisector between their seeds — its dual graph (connect neighboring seeds) is the Delaunay triangulation.
Bridge
RECAP → GAP: Voronoi answers "nearest seed" beautifully — but building it for a million points, every query, is expensive. A faster structure?
FILL: the k-d tree — recursively slice space in half, alternating axis, and prune whole halves during search.
KD-Tree (1/2)
Build: alternate the cutting axis at every depth
Depth 0 splits by x at the median point, depth 1 splits by y, depth 2 back to x — a balanced tree in O(n log n), each node owning one point and one rectangle of space.
KD-Tree (2/2)
Search: descend, backtrack, prune what can't win
Walk down to the query's leaf region for a first guess, then climb back up — only entering a sibling branch if its splitting plane is closer than the current best distance. Most branches get skipped entirely.
The whole map, in one frame
Points and vectors gave us orientation and the two products. Orientation gave us intersection and the hull. The hull and brute force gave us divide-and-conquer. Ray casting and sweeping gave us queries at scale. Voronoi and k-d trees gave us space itself, indexed.
