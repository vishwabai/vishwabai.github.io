Episode Five

CHAIN RULE

What happens when a function is stuffed inside another
function — and you need the rate of change of the whole thing.

Two things you already know

Composition, and the derivative

f(g(x)) — g runs first, feeds into f
f'(x) = lim(h→0) [f(x+h)−f(x)]/h

sin(x²) is exactly this: g(x)=x² runs first, then f(u)=sin(u) runs on the result. Neither rule you already have differentiates this directly — you need one more idea.

The core idea

Rates multiply through a chain

The inner machine's rate (du/dx) and the outer machine's rate (dy/du) don't add — they multiply to give the overall rate.

Reading the equation, piece by piece

dy/dx  =  dy/du  ·  du/dx

dy/dx — the thing we actually want: how fast the final output y changes as x changes
dy/du — the outer function's own rate: how fast y changes for a small change in its direct input, u
du/dx — the inner function's own rate: how fast u changes as x changes
Why this equation? · real-world use
Why it looks like fractions cancelling

Written in Leibniz notation, du seems to cancel: (dy/du)·(du/dx) = dy/dx, like ordinary fraction multiplication. That's a genuinely useful mnemonic — but du isn't literally a number here, so this isn't a proof. The proof comes from the limit definition, in the next slide.

Real-world use

This is exactly how unit conversions chain: km/hour × hour/day gives km/day — each rate cancels the shared unit in the middle, leaving only the outer units. The chain rule is that same "middle unit cancels" logic, applied to rates of change instead of unit conversions.

Why this equation? · real-world use
Why spot outer/inner first, always

Every chain-rule problem starts the same way: find the "last operation performed" (that's the outer function) and "everything inside it" (that's the inner function). Here, raising to the 5th power happens last, so u⁵ is outer and x²+1 is inner — get this identification right and the rest is just two easy derivatives multiplied together.

Real-world use

Compound growth formulas look exactly like this: (1 + rate)^time. Differentiating how a compounding quantity changes — investment value, population, radioactive decay — almost always means applying the chain rule to something raised to a power or an exponential, with a simpler expression nested inside.

Feynman's way of thinking about it

Two gears turning together

Turn the small gear (the inner function) at its own rate. It drives the big gear (the outer function) — but the big gear's speed depends on both its own size and how fast it's being driven. That combined speed is the chain rule.

Building intuition for the "why"

The mnemonic, done honestly

dy/du  ·  du/dx  =  dy/dx

The "du cancels" picture is a genuinely good way to remember the rule — but du and dx aren't ordinary numbers you can cancel by themselves. They're shorthand for a limiting process. The real justification is the derivation two slides back: multiplying two secant-slope ratios and taking the limit of the product.

Why this equation? · real-world use
Why the mnemonic is safe to use anyway

Even though "cancelling du" isn't a rigorous step on its own, the actual limit-based proof produces the exact same formula — so the shortcut always gives the right answer, even though the reasoning behind why is subtler than plain fraction cancellation.

Real-world use

Physicists and engineers lean on this "cancelling units" intuition constantly — converting between rates (m/s to km/hour), tracking how error propagates through a formula, or reasoning about dimensional analysis. It's an efficient shortcut precisely because it's backed by a real theorem, not just a coincidence of notation.

Watch out for

The mistake almost everyone makes first

forgetting du/dx

d/dx sin(x²) = cos(x²) is not the answer — you differentiated the outer shell and stopped. The inner derivative (2x) still needs to multiply in.

applying it when not needed

d/dx(x² + 3) needs no chain rule — nothing is nested inside anything. Chain rule is only for genuine composition, f(g(x)).

In Lagrange notation

The same rule, written the other way

[f(g(x))]'  =  f'(g(x))  ·  g'(x)

f'(g(x)) means: differentiate f as usual, then plug g(x) back in — not f'(x). That's the piece people mix up most often.
