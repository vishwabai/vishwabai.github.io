CHAPTER 0Orientation

The Big Picture

Before any formulas: what is this subject even for?

✗ What you think it is

"Linear algebra" sounds like a school subject about solving for x using grids of numbers called matrices — abstract, symbol-pushing, disconnected from anything real.

✓ What's actually true

It's the math of moving things around in a predictable, straight-line way — and it quietly runs your GPS, your photo filters, every recommendation engine, and every neural network you've ever touched.

Here's the one-sentence version a ten-year-old could get: linear algebra is the study of arrows — how to combine them, and how to build machines that move them around in straight, predictable ways. That's it. Every "advanced" idea later in this guide — matrices, determinants, eigenvectors — is just a more elaborate way of asking "where did the arrow go?"

🗺️ Why it matters — real problem #1

Your phone's GPS doesn't know your "location" the way you think of it. It knows a satellite signal as a vector — a direction and distance — and it solves a small system of linear equations to triangulate where those vectors must intersect. That triangulation is linear algebra running live in your pocket.

🧠 Why it matters — real problem #2

Every time a neural network "pays attention" to part of a sentence or image, it's doing one thing underneath: multiplying vectors by matrices, over and over. Strip away the buzzwords and a transformer is linear algebra wearing a trench coat.

▸ This animated map shows how each chapter is built only out of the chapter(s) before it — nothing is assumed that hasn't already been earned.

Reveal the why — why study this in order at all?
Most people who "learn linear algebra" memorize matrix arithmetic before they have any picture of what a matrix does. Then eigenvectors feel like a separate, scary chapter instead of the natural payoff of everything before it. We're doing it in dependency order on purpose: vectors → combining vectors → span/basis → matrices (which are just bundles of "where do basis vectors land") → multiplying matrices (chaining machines) → determinants (measuring the machine) → eigenvectors (the directions the machine respects). Each chapter is a tool built from the last one's parts.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

People expect linear algebra to feel like algebra — solving for a single unknown number. It's not. You're solving for shapes: whole sets of points, whole directions, whole spaces. The mental gear-shift from "find the number" to "find the shape" is the single biggest reason this subject feels disorienting at first. Hold onto that — every chapter ahead is really asking a geometry question dressed up in numbers.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

In a sentence or two: what is linear algebra actually the study of, and name one real system that secretly depends on it.

Model answer
Linear algebra studies arrows (vectors) and the rules for combining and transforming them in straight-line, predictable ways. GPS triangulation, image filters, and the matrix multiplications inside every neural network all depend on it directly.

Linear algebra = arrows + rules for combining them. Everything from here is that idea, elaborated.

CHAPTER 1Beginner

Vectors

An arrow with a direction and a size.

✗ What you think it is

A vector is a point in space — like the dot at coordinates (3, 4).

✓ What's actually true

A vector is a displacement — a "move 3 steps this way, 4 steps that way" instruction. It doesn't live anywhere in particular. The same vector can start at the origin, or start at your kitchen table — it's still the same arrow.

Picture giving someone directions: "walk 3 blocks east, then 4 blocks north." You didn't tell them where to start — you told them how to move. That move is a vector. We usually draw it starting at the origin just because it's convenient to compare arrows that way, not because the vector secretly lives there.

🚶 Analogy 1 — walking directions

"3 blocks east, 4 blocks north" is a vector. It has a direction (north-east-ish) and a size (the total distance you'd walk in a straight line — 5 blocks, as it happens). Two people who both walk "3 east, 4 north" end up the same distance and direction apart no matter where they each started.

🧾 Analogy 2 — the grocery list

A vector is also just an ordered list of numbers: (3 apples, 2 loaves of bread). No arrow needed — just quantities lined up in a fixed order. This is the "spreadsheet row" view, and it's exactly as valid as the arrow view. Engineers flip between both views constantly.

magnitude (length): 5.00

▸ Interactive lab 1/13 — drag the sliders. Notice the arrow's length (its magnitude) is computed by Pythagoras: √(x² + y²). The arrow never moves its tail — only where it points.

Reveal the why — why √(x² + y²)?
The arrow's horizontal run and vertical rise form the two legs of a right triangle; the arrow itself is the hypotenuse. That's the entire derivation — magnitude is just the Pythagorean theorem wearing a vector costume.
‖v‖ = √(x² + y²)    θ = arctan(y / x) Magnitude (length) and direction (angle from the positive x-axis) — the two numbers that fully describe a 2D vector, alongside its (x, y) components.
📐 Worked example — by hand
1
Take the vector v = (6, 8). Square each component. 6² = 36    8² = 64
2
Add the squares together. 36 + 64 = 100
3
Take the square root to get the magnitude. ‖v‖ = √100 = 10
4
Find the direction angle from the positive x-axis. θ = arctan(8/6) = arctan(1.333…) ≈ 53.13°
Result: v = (6, 8) has magnitude 10, pointing about 53.13° above the x-axis. This generalizes directly to 3D: for v = (x, y, z), ‖v‖ = √(x² + y² + z²) — just one more term added under the same square root, since 3D space gives you one more independent right-triangle leg to account for.
📷 Real-world hook: a GPS satellite doesn't send you a "location" — it sends a signal your phone turns into a distance-and-direction vector.
🎞️ Vectors generalize past 2D, too — 3D arrows behave by the exact same rules, just with one more number.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

Students get tripped up writing (3, 4) and assuming that means "the point at x=3, y=4." Sometimes it does mean a point! But when it's a vector, (3, 4) means "go 3 this way, 4 that way" — from wherever you currently are. The notation looks identical to a point's coordinates, and that visual collision is exactly why people conflate the two ideas for years.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Why is a vector not the same thing as a point, even though we write them the same way?

Model answer
A point is a fixed location. A vector is a displacement — an instruction for how far and which way to move — and that instruction is the same no matter where you start applying it. (3,4) as a point means "stand here." (3,4) as a vector means "move this much," regardless of where you started.
✅ Did you get it?
1. What is the magnitude of the vector (6, 8)?
2. Two vectors both equal (2, -1). One starts at the origin, the other starts at point (10, 10). Are they the same vector?
Key takeaway

A vector = direction + size, independent of where it's drawn. Its length is just Pythagoras: √(x²+y²).

CHAPTER 2Beginner

Adding & Scaling Vectors

The only two legal moves in the entire subject.

✗ What you think it is

Adding two vectors means adding their lengths — a 3-block walk plus a 4-block walk gives you a 7-block walk.

✓ What's actually true

Direction matters. You place the second arrow's tail at the first arrow's tip — tip-to-tail — and the sum is the arrow from the very start to the very end. Walking 3 blocks east then 4 blocks north does not put you 7 blocks from home — Pythagoras says 5.

Here is the entire rulebook of linear algebra, in full: you're allowed to add vectors (tip-to-tail), and you're allowed to scale them (stretch or shrink, possibly flipping direction with a negative number). That's the whole toolkit. Every later chapter — span, matrices, eigenvectors — is just these two moves used in clever combinations.

🚶‍♂️ Analogy 1 — relay directions

Adding vectors is like a relay race of instructions: "walk vector A, then from there walk vector B." Wherever you end up is A + B. It doesn't matter if you walk A first or B first — you land in the same final spot either way.

🎚️ Analogy 2 — a volume knob

Scaling a vector is a volume knob on direction: turning it up (multiply by 2) doubles the arrow's length without changing where it points. Turning the knob negative flips the arrow to point the opposite way. The direction itself never bends — only the strength.

A = (3, 1) + B = (1, 3) = (4, 4)

Reveal the why — why does order not matter for addition?
Walk A then B, or walk B then A — both routes trace two sides of the same parallelogram, and both end at the opposite corner. Geometrically there's only one "opposite corner," so A+B and B+A must land in the same place. This is why vector addition, like ordinary number addition, is commutative.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

People keep wanting to add vectors "component-by-component but also add the lengths separately," double-counting the geometry. You never need to touch lengths directly — add the x's, add the y's, and the resulting length falls out automatically from Pythagoras on the new (x, y). Trust the components; don't manually wrangle the length.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Describe tip-to-tail addition, and what scaling by a negative number does.

Model answer
Tip-to-tail addition means placing the second vector's starting point at the first vector's ending point; the sum is the single arrow from the very first start to the very last end. Scaling by a negative number reverses the arrow's direction while keeping it on the same line, and shrinks or grows its length by the scalar's absolute value.

Key takeaway

Two legal moves only: add (tip-to-tail, by components) and scale (stretch/shrink/flip). Everything else in the subject is built from just these two.

CHAPTER 3Intermediate

Span & Basis

Every place a set of vectors can possibly reach.

✗ What you think it is

If you have some vectors, their "span" is just... whatever space they're drawn in. A 2D vector's span is "all of 2D," obviously.

✓ What's actually true

Span is only the points you can reach by adding and scaling the specific vectors you were given. One lone 2D vector spans only a line through the origin — not the whole plane. Two vectors only span the whole plane if they point in genuinely different directions; if they're parallel, you're still stuck on a line, no matter how many of them you throw in.

Mixing paint is the cleanest way to feel this. If you only own red and yellow paint, you can mix any orange you like, but you will never, ever produce blue — red and yellow don't span blue. Vectors work the same way: the combinations you can build (a·v₁ + b·v₂ for any numbers a, b) are your span, and nothing outside that set is reachable, ever.

🎨 Analogy 1 — mixing paint

Span = every color you can mix from the cans you own. Two non-matching primary colors can reach a whole gamut (a 2D span); two cans of the exact same color can only ever make darker or lighter versions of that one color (a 1D span, a line).

🔑 Analogy 2 — the minimal toolkit

A basis is the smallest, leanest set of vectors that still spans everything you need — no redundant tools in the bag. In 2D, any two non-parallel vectors make a perfectly good basis: with just those two, plus scaling and adding, you can reach every single point on the plane.

▸ Interactive lab 4/13 — every (a, b) combination drops a dot at a·v₁ + b·v₂. Sweep the sliders and the dots fill the whole plane. Click "parallel vectors" to watch the reachable set collapse onto a single line — that's a span that has gone from 2D down to 1D.

This collapsing is exactly linear independence in disguise: v₁ and v₂ are independent if neither is just a scaled copy of the other — i.e. if dropping either one actually shrinks what you can reach. The moment one vector is redundant (a scaled copy of another), it contributes nothing new to the span.

⚡ Common Sticking Point

The phrase "linearly independent" sounds like it's describing the vectors' lengths or values — it isn't. It's purely about whether one vector is reachable as a combination of the others. A vector can be enormous, tiny, or weirdly shaped; none of that affects independence. Only "is it redundant with the others" matters.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Why do two parallel vectors only span a line, even though you can scale each one as much as you like?

Model answer
Scaling a vector only moves you along the line it already points on — it never rotates it. If a second vector points in that exact same direction (parallel), adding scaled copies of it still only moves you further along that same line. No combination of two parallel vectors can ever produce a sideways step off that line, so the span stays one-dimensional.
✅ Did you get it?
1. v₁ = (1, 2) and v₂ = (2, 4). What is span{v₁, v₂}?
2. What's the minimum number of vectors needed to form a basis for ordinary 2D space?
Key takeaway

Span = everywhere reachable by combining your vectors. A basis is the smallest independent set that still reaches everywhere you need.

CHAPTER 4Intermediate

Matrices as Machines

A grid of numbers that secretly means "move every point like this."

✗ What you think it is

A matrix is a rectangular grid of numbers you memorize multiplication rules for — abstract bookkeeping.

✓ What's actually true

A matrix is a transformation — a rule for moving every single point in space at once. The numbers in the grid are nothing more than instructions: "here's where the basis vectors land." Once you know where your basis vectors go, you automatically know where every other point goes too.

Remember your basis vectors from the last chapter — the minimal toolkit, usually written î = (1,0) and ĵ = (0,1). A matrix is just a record of where those two arrows end up after the transformation. The first column of the matrix tells you where î lands; the second column tells you where ĵ lands. Once you know that, every other vector's new location is just a linear combination of those two new landing spots.

🌀 Analogy 1 — the vector blender

A matrix is a kitchen appliance: vectors go in one end, transformed vectors come out the other. Like a blender, it does the exact same operation no matter what you feed it — it's a fixed rule, applied uniformly to all of space.

📸 Analogy 2 — an Instagram filter

A photo filter applies one consistent rule to every pixel — stretch the reds, rotate the hues. A matrix is the same idea applied to coordinates instead of colors: one fixed rule, applied identically everywhere in space.

▸ Interactive lab 5/13 — drag any of the four sliders and watch the entire grid warp. The teal arrow is where î ends up; the amber arrow is where ĵ ends up. Those two columns of numbers ARE the matrix.

Each column of a matrix is literally just the coordinates of where one basis vector ends up. Column 1 = new home of î. Column 2 = new home of ĵ. There's no deeper mystery than that.
Reveal the why — why does knowing where î and ĵ land tell you everything?
Any vector (x, y) is secretly the linear combination x·î + y·ĵ. Since a matrix transformation respects addition and scaling (that's what "linear" means), the transformed vector must be x·(new î) + y·(new ĵ). So once you know where the two basis vectors land, every other point's destination is just arithmetic — no new information required.
🎞️ A rotation matrix in motion — exactly the m₁₁..m₂₂ sliders above, animated continuously instead of by hand.
📷 Every GPU shader that rotates, scales, or skews a 3D model is running matrix-vector multiplication, this exact operation, millions of times per second.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

People stare at a matrix as a static table and try to memorize "multiplication rules" by rote. The unlock is to stop seeing rows first — see columns first, and read each column as a destination point for a basis vector. Once you can glance at a matrix and immediately picture the deformed grid, the arithmetic becomes a formality instead of a mystery.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

What does each column of a matrix actually represent?

Model answer
Each column records where one basis vector lands after the transformation. The first column is the new position of î, the second is the new position of ĵ (and so on in higher dimensions). The matrix as a whole is a compact description of "where does each basis direction go," and that's enough to describe where every point in space goes.
✅ Did you get it?
1. A matrix has first column (0, 1) and second column (-1, 0). Where does î = (1,0) land?
2. The "identity matrix" leaves every vector exactly where it started. What must its two columns be?
Key takeaway

A matrix's columns are just the new landing spots of the basis vectors. That's the whole secret of what a matrix "is."

CHAPTER 5Advanced

Systems & Matrix Multiplication

Solving for unknowns, and chaining machines together.

✗ What you think it is

Multiplying two matrices means multiplying matching cells together, like multiplying two spreadsheets entry-by-entry.

✓ What's actually true

Matrix multiplication is composition — "do transformation B, then do transformation A to the result." It's the same idea as applying two Instagram filters back to back. And because order of operations matters in real life (rotate-then-stretch looks different from stretch-then-rotate), matrix multiplication is not commutative: A×B usually does not equal B×A.

A system of equations like "2x + y = 5, x − y = 1" is secretly just one matrix equation: a transformation matrix M times an unknown vector (x, y) lands on a known target vector (5, 1). Solving the system means asking: "what input, when run through this machine, produces this exact output?" Chapter 6 will show you the clean way to reverse a machine; for now we'll solve it directly with elimination, the same technique you may already know from school — just relabeled with its geometric meaning.

🥪 Analogy 1 — sandwich assembly line

Matrix multiplication is two machines bolted together on an assembly line: a vector enters machine B first, the output of B feeds straight into machine A. The combined "super-machine" A×B is itself just another matrix — work out its columns and you've described the whole pipeline in one grid.

🧩 Analogy 2 — reverse-engineering a route

Solving Mx = b is like knowing the destination and the rules of the road, and working backward to figure out which starting point and turns got you there. Elimination is one systematic way to walk that path backward, equation by equation.

Gaussian elimination — solving 2x + y = 5, x − y = 1

▸ Interactive lab 6/13 — step through the elimination one move at a time. Each step is a legal "row operation": you're allowed to scale a row, or add a multiple of one row to another, without changing the solution.

Reveal the why — why are row operations "legal" (they don't change the answer)?
Each equation describes the exact same line of solutions. Scaling an equation by a nonzero number, or adding a multiple of one equation to another, produces a new equation that's still satisfied by exactly the same (x, y) pairs — you've just described the same constraint differently. Nothing about the actual solution set has changed; you've only relabeled it into a more convenient form.
(AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ Aᵢₖ Bₖⱼ The general matrix multiplication formula: entry (i, j) of the product is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B. This is just bookkeeping for "feed column j of B through transformation A," repeated for every column.
📐 Worked example — multiplying two matrices by hand
1
Multiply A = [2 0; 1 3] by B = [1 4; 2 0]. Start with entry (1,1): row 1 of A dotted with column 1 of B. (AB)₁₁ = (2)(1) + (0)(2) = 2
2
Entry (1,2): row 1 of A dotted with column 2 of B. (AB)₁₂ = (2)(4) + (0)(0) = 8
3
Entry (2,1): row 2 of A dotted with column 1 of B. (AB)₂₁ = (1)(1) + (3)(2) = 7
4
Entry (2,2): row 2 of A dotted with column 2 of B. (AB)₂₂ = (1)(4) + (3)(0) = 4
Result: AB = [2 8; 7 4]. Try BA by hand and you'll get a different answer entirely — concrete proof that matrix multiplication doesn't commute, exactly as the chapter's misconception box warned.
🎞️ Elimination, sped up: this is the same row-juggling your computer does internally — just far faster, and for far bigger grids.
Composing two matrices:

0-110 × 2002 = 0-220

"rotate 90°" composed with "scale by 2" — try swapping the order on paper and you'll land on a different (though related) result.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

The instinct to multiply matrices "entry by entry" (like adding) is so strong that almost everyone tries it at least once. It fails because multiplication isn't measuring "similarity cell by cell" — it's asking "if I run a vector through B first and then through A, where does it end up." The row-times-column dot-product rule is just bookkeeping for that two-step journey; it's worth re-deriving it from the composition idea rather than memorizing it cold.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Why isn't A×B generally the same as B×A?

Model answer
A×B means "apply B first, then apply A" to a vector. Doing transformation B then A is generally a different journey through space than doing A then B — the same way rotating a piece of paper then folding it gives a different result than folding it first and then rotating. Order changes the path, so it usually changes the destination.

CHAPTER 6Advanced

Determinant & Inverse

How much a machine stretches space — and how to run it backwards.

✗ What you think it is

The determinant is some scary formula — ad minus bc — that you compute and then promptly forget, with no intuitive meaning.

✓ What's actually true

The determinant is simply the area scale factor of the transformation. The unit square (area = 1) gets mapped to some parallelogram, and the determinant is exactly that parallelogram's area. A determinant of 3 means "this machine triples every area." A determinant of 0 means the machine squashes space flat — and a flattened machine can never be run backwards, which is precisely why it has no inverse.

det = 1.40

▸ Interactive lab 7/13 — drag any slider until the shaded parallelogram visibly flattens into a line. Watch the det readout hit exactly zero at that exact moment — that is not a coincidence.

BEFORE understanding — the formula view

det = m₁₁·m₂₂ − m₁₂·m₂₁. Plug in numbers, get a number, move on. No picture, no meaning — just a rule to apply.

AFTER understanding — the geometry view

det = the signed area of the parallelogram that the unit square turns into. The formula above is just the area formula for a parallelogram, rewritten in terms of the matrix's columns. A negative determinant means the transformation also flips the plane over, like turning a glove inside out.

🥞 Analogy 1 — a pasta roller

Run dough through a pasta roller set to "thin," and the dough's area grows (det > 1). Crank it the wrong way and squish the dough into a flat sheet with zero thickness (det = 0) — and a flattened sheet of dough can never be un-squished back into its original shape. That irreversibility is exactly why det = 0 means "no inverse."

↩️ Analogy 2 — undo button

The inverse matrix is simply "undo." If matrix M moves î and ĵ somewhere, M⁻¹ is the machine that moves them right back. It exists only when M didn't destroy any information along the way — which is exactly when det(M) ≠ 0.

Reveal the why — why does det = 0 mean "no inverse"?
If a transformation squashes the plane down onto a line (or a line down onto a point), then many different starting points all land on the very same output point. "Undoing" that output would require knowing which of the many possible starting points to send it back to — but that information was destroyed the moment everything got squashed together. No rule can recover information that no longer exists, so no inverse can be built.
M⁻¹ = (1 / det(M)) · [ m₂₂  −m₁₂ ; −m₂₁  m₁₁ ] The 2×2 inverse formula: swap the diagonal entries, negate the off-diagonal entries, then divide everything by the determinant. If det(M) = 0, this formula tries to divide by zero — which is exactly the algebraic fingerprint of "no inverse exists."
📐 Worked example — inverting a matrix by hand
1
Take M = [2 1; 1 1] (columns (2,1) and (1,1)). Compute the determinant first. det(M) = (2)(1) − (1)(1) = 2 − 1 = 1
2
Since det(M) = 1 ≠ 0, an inverse exists. Swap the diagonal entries (2 and 1 trade places) and negate the off-diagonal entries. swapped/negated = [1 −1; −1 2]
3
Divide every entry by det(M) = 1 (here it changes nothing, since dividing by 1 is a no-op). M⁻¹ = [1 −1; −1 2]
4
Sanity-check: multiplying M by M⁻¹ should give the identity matrix. M · M⁻¹ = [2(1)+1(−1)  2(−1)+1(2); 1(1)+1(−1)  1(−1)+1(2)] = [1 0; 0 1] ✓
Result: M⁻¹ = [1 −1; −1 2]. The check confirms it — multiplying a matrix by its inverse always undoes the transformation exactly, landing back on the identity.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

Students memorize "det = 0 means no solution" without connecting it to squashing. The cleanest fix: whenever you see det = 0, immediately picture the grid in the lab above going flat. A flattened grid can't be un-flattened — that single mental image replaces an entire page of memorized rules about singular matrices.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

What does it mean, geometrically, for a matrix to have determinant zero?

Model answer
A determinant of zero means the transformation collapses space into a lower dimension — a plane onto a line, or a line onto a point — so the resulting area (or volume) is zero. Because multiple distinct inputs get squashed onto the same output, the transformation destroys information and cannot be reversed, which is why it has no inverse.

CHAPTER 7Expert / Synthesis

Eigenvectors & Eigenvalues

The directions a machine never bends — only stretches.

✗ What you think it is

"Eigenvectors" sounds like a separate, advanced, intimidating topic bolted onto the end of the course — unrelated to anything you've learned so far.

✓ What's actually true

An eigenvector is just a direction that a given matrix doesn't rotate — it only stretches or shrinks it (by a factor called the eigenvalue). You already have every tool needed to find one: it's a vector v and a number λ such that Mv = λv. That's a matrix-vector multiply on the left, and ordinary scaling on the right — nothing new at all, just the chapter-4 machine and the chapter-2 scaling move, pointed at each other.

Most transformations rotate most directions at least a little. But for almost every matrix, there exist one or two very special directions where, no matter how hard the matrix pulls, the output still points exactly along the same line as the input — only longer, shorter, or flipped. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor along each one is its eigenvalue.

🪵 Analogy 1 — the grain of wood

Push a plank of wood and it might twist and bend in most directions — except along the grain, where it only compresses or stretches cleanly, with no twisting. Eigenvectors are the "grain" of a transformation: the directions where the deformation is pure, simple scaling.

🌀 Analogy 2 — the eye of a spinning storm

In a swirling transformation, most points get dragged sideways and rotated. But certain special axes pass straight through, getting stretched along their own length without curving — like the still axis at the center of a hurricane while everything around it swirls.

▸ Interactive lab 8/13 — the two magenta lines are the eigen-directions for the current matrix. Press animate and watch every other direction swing and curve, while those two lines only stretch in place. Try the rotation preset — a pure rotation turns every direction, so it has no real eigenvectors at all (their eigenvalues become "imaginary," a story for another day).

Reveal the why — why does this matter for machine learning?
PCA (dimensionality reduction) finds the eigenvectors of a data's covariance matrix — they're literally the "directions of maximum spread" in your dataset, found by this exact same Mv = λv idea. Google's original PageRank algorithm ranked the entire web by finding the dominant eigenvector of a giant link matrix. And inside a transformer, every attention step is built from matrix multiplications and dot products — the same operations you just spent seven chapters building from scratch. Eigenvectors aren't a separate topic from your day-to-day ML work; they're the same handful of moves you already know, applied to bigger matrices.
det(M − λI) = 0    ⟹    λ² − tr(M)λ + det(M) = 0 For a 2×2 matrix, the eigenvalue equation always boils down to this quadratic — called the characteristic polynomial — where tr(M) = m₁₁ + m₂₂ is the trace (sum of diagonal entries). Solve it with the ordinary quadratic formula, and each root λ is one eigenvalue.
📐 Worked example — finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand
1
Take M = [3 1; 0 2] (columns (3,0) and (1,2)). Write out det(M − λI) = 0. det([3−λ  1; 0  2−λ]) = (3−λ)(2−λ) − (1)(0) = 0
2
Expand and solve the quadratic for λ. (3−λ)(2−λ) = 0  ⟹  λ = 3  or  λ = 2
3
For λ₁ = 3, substitute back into (M − λI)v = 0 and solve for v. [0 1; 0 −1]v = 0  ⟹  v₁ = (1, 0)
4
For λ₂ = 2, do the same substitution again. [1 1; 0 0]v = 0  ⟹  v₂ = (1, −1)
Result: M = [3 1; 0 2] has eigenvalue λ₁=3 with eigenvector (1,0), and eigenvalue λ₂=2 with eigenvector (1,−1). Check: M(1,0) = (3,0) = 3·(1,0) ✓, and M(1,−1) = (3−1, −2) = (2,−2) = 2·(1,−1) ✓.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

The equation Mv = λv looks like it should be "solve for v," but v appears on both sides, which feels circular. The trick that unlocks it: rewrite it as (M − λI)v = 0. For a nonzero v to satisfy that, the matrix (M − λI) must squash space — meaning its determinant must be zero. That single observation (det(M − λI) = 0) turns "guess the special direction" into "solve a normal equation for λ," which is exactly why Chapter 6's determinant chapter had to come before this one.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

In your own words: what is an eigenvector, and why does a pure rotation matrix not have any real ones?

Model answer
An eigenvector is a direction that a matrix only stretches or shrinks, never rotates off its own line, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. A pure rotation turns every single direction by the same angle, so there is no direction left unrotated — meaning no real eigenvectors exist for a genuine rotation (other than the trivial zero-degree case).

CHAPTER 8Expert

Dot Product & Projection

Measuring how much two arrows agree.

✗ What you think it is

The dot product is a weird arithmetic trick — multiply matching components and add them up — with no real meaning, just a formula to memorize for tests.

✓ What's actually true

The dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way. A big positive dot product means they largely agree in direction; zero means they're perfectly perpendicular; negative means they're pointing into opposite halves of the world. It is, quietly, the single most-used operation in every machine learning system — every "attention score," every "similarity," every neuron's weighted input is a dot product.

Here's the cleanest way to feel it: take one vector and shine a flashlight straight down onto another, casting a shadow. The dot product (after a little rescaling) is the length of that shadow. If the two vectors point the same way, the shadow is as long as possible. If they're perpendicular, the shadow disappears to a single point — length zero.

🔦 Analogy 1 — casting a shadow

Shine a light directly above vector B, perpendicular to B's direction. Vector A casts a shadow onto B's line. That shadow's length, scaled by B's length, is A·B. Tilt A away from B and the shadow shrinks; once A is perpendicular to B, the shadow vanishes completely — the dot product hits exactly zero.

⛵ Analogy 2 — wind and sail

A sailboat only catches the part of the wind that pushes along its direction of travel; a crosswind perpendicular to the boat contributes nothing to forward motion. The dot product of the wind vector and the boat's heading vector is exactly that "useful component" — wasted sideways force doesn't count.

A·B = 3.54

▸ Interactive lab 9/13 — B stays fixed; A rotates. Watch the dashed shadow of A on B's line shrink to nothing at exactly 90°, then turn negative past that as A starts pointing backward relative to B.

Reveal the why — where does A·B = |A||B|cos(θ) come from?
The shadow of A onto B's direction has length |A|·cos(θ), where θ is the angle between them — that's just basic right-triangle trigonometry. Multiply that shadow length by |B| (a convenient rescaling that also makes the component-wise formula work out) and you get |A||B|cos(θ). It can be proven that this is exactly equal to multiplying matching components and summing — a·c + b·d for A=(a,b), B=(c,d) — but the "shadow times length" picture is the one that actually carries meaning.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

People are surprised a dot product can be negative. It feels like "lengths multiplied together" should always be positive. But the dot product isn't just multiplying lengths — it's multiplying lengths by cos(θ), and cosine goes negative past 90°. A negative dot product simply means the angle between the vectors is obtuse — they're leaning away from each other, not that anything was measured "wrong."

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

What does a dot product of exactly zero tell you about two vectors?

Model answer
A dot product of zero means the two vectors are perpendicular (orthogonal) — neither has any component along the other's direction, so the "shadow" one casts on the other has zero length.

CHAPTER 9Expert

Rank & Null Space

How many dimensions a machine's output actually fills — and which inputs it secretly destroys.

✗ What you think it is

"Rank" is just another word for the size of a matrix — a 3×3 matrix has "rank 3," end of story.

✓ What's actually true

Rank is the number of dimensions the transformation's output actually fills — which can be smaller than the matrix's size. A 2×2 matrix that squashes the whole plane onto a single line has rank 1, no matter how big the numbers inside it are. And every input direction that gets squashed all the way down to the origin — sent to exactly (0,0) — belongs to the matrix's null space.

You already met this idea wearing a different hat back in Chapter 6: a determinant of zero meant the transformation squashes space flat. Rank and null space are just the more precise, more general language for the very same phenomenon — rank tells you exactly how much dimension survives, and the null space tells you exactly which inputs got erased along the way.

🥞 Analogy 1 — the pancake press

Picture a 3D lump of dough pressed by a flat plate. If the plate squashes it into a flat pancake, the output only fills 2 of the original 3 dimensions — rank 2. Squash harder into a thin rope, and you're down to rank 1. Anything that got pressed straight down to a single point belongs to the null space — that "vertical" direction the press destroyed entirely.

📻 Analogy 2 — a lossy radio signal

A transformation with full rank passes every bit of input information through untouched, like a perfect radio signal. A rank-deficient transformation is a lossy channel: some combinations of inputs get crushed into static (the null space) before they ever reach the output, and there's no way for the receiver to recover what was lost.

rank = 1

▸ Interactive lab 10/13 — the starting sliders already make a rank-1 matrix (column 2 is a scaled copy of column 1). The red dashed line marks the null space: every input vector along that line gets sent all the way to the origin. Nudge a slider off the rank-1 setting and watch rank jump to 2 and the null space shrink to just the origin point.

Reveal the why — why is the null space always a line through the origin (not some random spot)?
Scaling preserves "being sent to zero": if Mv = 0, then M(kv) = k(Mv) = k·0 = 0 for any scalar k. So if one vector is destroyed, every scaled copy of it is destroyed too — meaning the null space is never just a single rogue vector, it's always a whole line (or plane, in higher dimensions) through the origin. This follows directly from the same "linearity" rules from Chapter 2 — nothing new was assumed.
rank(M) + dim(null space of M) = n The Rank-Nullity Theorem: for an n-column matrix, the dimensions that survive (rank) plus the dimensions that get destroyed (nullity) always add up to exactly n — nothing more, nothing less. In the chapter's lab, n=2: a rank-1 matrix has a 1-dimensional null space (1+1=2); a rank-2 (full rank) matrix has a 0-dimensional null space, just the origin (2+0=2).
⚡ Common Sticking Point

It's tempting to think "rank" describes the matrix's size or its biggest numbers. It doesn't — a matrix full of huge numbers can still have rank 1 if its columns are scaled copies of each other. Rank is purely about independence between columns (echoing Chapter 3's linear independence), never about magnitude. Always ask "how many genuinely different directions do these columns point in," not "how big are these numbers."

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

What is the null space of a matrix, and why is it always a line (or plane) through the origin rather than some other shape?

Model answer
The null space is the set of all input vectors a matrix sends to exactly the origin. It's always a line or plane through the origin (never an isolated point or curve) because scaling any vector that maps to zero produces another vector that also maps to zero, by linearity — so the destroyed inputs always form a full subspace, not just a stray point.

Key takeaway

CHAPTER 10Expert

Norms & Distance

Different honest ways to measure "how big" a vector is.

✗ What you think it is

"Length of a vector" has exactly one obvious meaning — the straight-line ruler distance, computed with Pythagoras. Why would you ever need another definition?

✓ What's actually true

Straight-line distance (called the L2 norm) is only one of several legitimate ways to measure size, and different problems genuinely call for different rulers. The L1 norm (add up the absolute values of the components — "city block" distance) and the L∞ norm (just take the single largest component) are both equally valid measuring sticks, each better suited to certain real problems than ordinary straight-line distance.

Picture a taxi in Manhattan. It can't cut diagonally through buildings — it has to drive along the grid, east-west then north-south. The "Manhattan distance" it actually travels is not the straight diagonal ruler distance; it's the sum of the horizontal and vertical blocks: |Δx| + |Δy|. That's the L1 norm, and it's exactly as legitimate a notion of "distance" as the diagonal one — it's just answering a different question.

🚕 Analogy 1 — the Manhattan taxi (L1)

A taxi confined to a street grid measures distance as blocks traveled: |Δx| + |Δy|, never the diagonal shortcut a bird could take. This L1 norm shows up constantly in machine learning whenever you want to penalize "any nonzero usage" heavily and encourage sparse, all-or-nothing solutions.

🏔️ Analogy 2 — the mountaineer's worst leg (L∞)

If a hiking trip has several legs of different lengths, the L∞ norm only cares about the single longest, hardest leg — max(|x|, |y|, ...) — ignoring how short the easy legs were. It's the "your trip is only as good as your worst stretch" measure of size.

vector = (4, 3)

▸ Interactive lab 11/13 — for the fixed vector (4, 3): the L2 ruler distance is 5, the L1 taxi distance is 7 (4 blocks + 3 blocks), and the L∞ distance is just 4 (the single largest component). All three numbers are "correct" answers to slightly different questions about size.

This generalizes cleanly: the p-norm family is ‖v‖ₚ = (|x₁|ᵖ + |x₂|ᵖ + ... )^(1/p). Set p=1 and you get the taxi-block L1 norm. Set p=2 and the formula collapses exactly into ordinary Pythagorean distance, the L2 norm you met all the way back in Chapter 1. Push p toward infinity and the formula quietly turns into "just the biggest component" — the L∞ norm. One formula, three (in fact infinitely many) honest rulers, depending on which exponent you choose.

Reveal the why — why does anyone actually use L1 instead of the "obvious" L2?
In machine learning, penalizing a model's weights with the L1 norm tends to push many weights to exactly zero, producing sparse, simple models — useful for automatic feature selection. The L2 norm, by contrast, shrinks weights smoothly toward zero without usually hitting it exactly. The geometric reason traces back to the shape each norm's "unit ball" takes: L1's unit ball is a diamond with sharp corners on the axes, and optimization solutions tend to land precisely on those sharp corners — which is exactly where a coordinate equals zero.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

Students assume "distance" must mean the straight-line ruler measurement, because that's the only one taught in school geometry. The unlock is realizing that "distance" is really a contract — any function satisfying a short list of common-sense rules (never negative, only zero when comparing something to itself, and the triangle inequality) legally qualifies as a distance. The straight ruler is just the most familiar member of a much larger, equally legitimate family.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Why might a machine learning engineer deliberately choose the L1 norm over the more familiar L2 norm?

Model answer
The L1 norm tends to push some values all the way to exactly zero rather than just shrinking them, which produces sparse, simpler results — useful when you want a model to "select" only a few important features and ignore the rest entirely, something the smoother L2 norm doesn't reliably do.

Key takeaway

The p-norm family generalizes "length": L1 sums absolute values (taxi distance), L2 is ordinary Pythagorean distance, L∞ takes just the largest component. Different problems genuinely call for different ones.

CHAPTER 11Expert / Synthesis

Change of Basis

The same arrow, described in a completely different coordinate language.

✗ What you think it is

The numbers (3, 4) describing a vector are a fixed, objective fact about that arrow — there's only one correct way to write its coordinates.

✓ What's actually true

The numbers (3, 4) only make sense relative to a chosen basis — usually the everyday î=(1,0), ĵ=(0,1) pair. Pick a different basis (say, two tilted, stretched arrows instead) and the very same physical arrow gets a completely different numeric description. The arrow in the real world never moves — only the "language" you're describing it in changes.

This is the same idea as measuring temperature in Celsius versus Fahrenheit: 0°C and 32°F describe the exact same physical state of water, just in two different number systems. A change of basis is the vector-world version of that conversion — and remarkably, a matrix (the very same tool from Chapter 4) is exactly the right device to translate coordinates from one basis's language into another's.

🌡️ Analogy 1 — Celsius vs. Fahrenheit

Water freezes at the same physical temperature no matter what you call it. Celsius and Fahrenheit are two different "bases" for describing temperature, and converting between them is a simple linear formula — directly analogous to converting a vector's coordinates from one basis to another.

🗺️ Analogy 2 — grid north vs. true north

A hiker's compass bearing depends on which "north" you measure against — magnetic north and true north disagree by a few degrees depending on location. The hiker's actual direction of travel never changes; only the numeric bearing does, depending on the reference grid (basis) chosen.

As you tilt that new basis, the arrow's coordinates in that basis (printed at right) change continuously — even though the arrow itself hasn't budged an inch in the real world.

Reveal the why — why does this matter for real systems?
Eigenvectors from Chapter 7 are so useful precisely because, if you change basis to align with them, a complicated matrix becomes a simple diagonal one — multiplication along each axis independently, with no cross-terms. That diagonalization trick (eigendecomposition) is the computational engine behind PCA, behind efficiently computing high powers of a matrix, and behind a huge share of numerical linear algebra in machine learning. Change of basis isn't a side note — it's the move that makes hard problems become easy ones, just by choosing better coordinates.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

It feels deeply uncomfortable that the "same" vector can have wildly different-looking coordinates. The fix is to firmly separate the vector itself (a real, physical arrow that doesn't care about your coordinate choices) from its coordinate representation (a list of numbers that's entirely dependent on which basis you picked). Confusing the arrow with its current numeric label is the single most common conceptual trap in this whole chapter.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

Why can the exact same physical vector have two completely different coordinate pairs?

Model answer
Coordinates are always relative to a chosen basis, not an inherent property of the vector itself. The vector is a fixed physical arrow; its numeric coordinates are just a description written in the "language" of whichever basis you pick. Change the basis (the language) and the coordinates (the description) change, even though the underlying arrow never moves.

CHAPTER 12Expert / Capstone

Diagonalization

The payoff: rewriting a matrix as A = PDP⁻¹.

✗ What you think it is

Diagonalization is a separate, optional party trick — a formula A = PDP⁻¹ you memorize for an exam and then never see again.

✓ What's actually true

Diagonalization is the literal capstone of everything in this guide: it's eigenvectors (Chapter 7) used as a change of basis (Chapter 11) to rewrite a messy matrix as pure, independent scaling along each new axis. Once diagonalized, a transformation that looked tangled in the original coordinates becomes nothing more than "stretch axis 1 by λ₁, stretch axis 2 by λ₂" — no rotation, no shear, no cross-terms.

Here is the whole idea in one line: if a matrix M has eigenvectors v₁ and v₂ with eigenvalues λ₁ and λ₂, build P by using those eigenvectors as its columns, and build D as the diagonal matrix of their eigenvalues. Then M = PDP⁻¹. Read right to left: P⁻¹ first translates ordinary coordinates into the "eigenvector basis" language (Chapter 11), D then does trivial independent stretching along those axes (just multiply each coordinate by its own λ — no cross-terms, no mixing), and P translates back out into ordinary coordinates. Three simple, already-familiar steps standing in for one originally complicated transformation.

🗝️ Analogy 1 — translate, simplify, translate back

Imagine a tricky physics problem that's only hard because you're using the wrong coordinate axes — tilt your head to align with the object's natural axes and the problem suddenly becomes trivial. P⁻¹ is "tilt your head," D is "now the trivial problem," and P is "tilt back" so the answer is reported in everyone else's normal coordinates.

⚡ Analogy 2 — repeated stretching, made cheap

Computing M¹⁰⁰ directly means multiplying matrices together 99 times — expensive and error-prone. But Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹, and raising a diagonal matrix to the nth power just means raising each diagonal entry to the nth power independently. Diagonalization turns a brutal repeated-multiplication problem into a handful of scalar exponents.

▸ Interactive lab 13/13 — using M with eigenvectors (1,0) and (0,1) and eigenvalues λ₁=2, λ₂=0.5: step through P⁻¹ → D → P individually, or watch the whole composed journey at once. Notice the grid only ever undergoes pure, uncrossed stretching once it's in the eigenbasis.

Reveal the why — why must the eigenvectors go in P, and the eigenvalues in D, in matching order?
Recall Chapter 4: a matrix's columns record where each basis vector lands. P's first column is v₁, meaning P sends the "standard" first basis direction directly onto v₁ — exactly the translation we need going from the eigenbasis into ordinary coordinates. D is built to scale the first coordinate by λ₁ and the second by λ₂ — but that scaling only does the right thing if "first coordinate" already means "amount of v₁," which is precisely what P⁻¹ arranges first. Mismatch the order between P's columns and D's diagonal entries, and the whole composition multiplies the wrong eigenvalue onto the wrong eigenvector.
📐 Worked example — full diagonalization, start to finish
1
Reuse M = [3 1; 0 2] from Chapter 7, where we already found λ₁=3 with v₁=(1,0), and λ₂=2 with v₂=(1,−1). Build P from the eigenvectors as columns, and D from the eigenvalues on the diagonal. P = [1 1; 0 −1]    D = [3 0; 0 2]
2
Invert P using the Chapter 6 formula: det(P) = (1)(−1) − (1)(0) = −1. P⁻¹ = (1/−1)[−1 −1; 0 1] = [1 1; 0 −1]
3
Multiply P · D (apply D's scaling, then P's basis-translation). PD = [1(3)+1(0)  1(0)+1(2); 0(3)+(−1)(0)  0(0)+(−1)(2)] = [3 2; 0 −2]
4
Multiply that result by P⁻¹ to confirm it reconstructs the original M. (PD)P⁻¹ = [3(1)+2(0)  3(1)+2(−1); 0(1)+(−2)(0)  0(1)+(−2)(−1)] = [3 1; 0 2] = M ✓
Result: M = PDP⁻¹ checks out exactly, recovering the original [3 1; 0 2]. Now M⁵ is cheap: instead of multiplying M by itself four more times, just compute PD⁵P⁻¹, where D⁵ = [3⁵ 0; 0 2⁵] = [243 0; 0 32] — three trivial scalar exponents standing in for repeated 2×2 matrix multiplication.
⚡ Common Sticking Point

Not every matrix can be diagonalized — specifically, ones without enough independent eigenvectors to fill out a full basis (you met one such case back in Chapter 7's shear preset, which has only one independent eigen-direction in 2D, not two). When P can't be built from a full set of independent columns, P⁻¹ doesn't exist, and the whole PDP⁻¹ trick collapses. This isn't a glitch — it's the same "not enough independent directions" idea from Chapter 3 and Chapter 9, showing up one final time.

🗣️ Now explain it in your own words

In your own words, what do P, D, and P⁻¹ each individually accomplish in the equation M = PDP⁻¹?

Model answer
P⁻¹ converts ordinary coordinates into "how much of each eigenvector" coordinates. D then scales each of those eigen-coordinates independently by its own eigenvalue, with no mixing between them. P converts the result back out of eigen-coordinates into ordinary coordinates. Chained together, the three steps reproduce exactly what the original matrix M does, just routed through a basis where the actual work (D) is trivial.
✅ Did you get it?
1. If M = PDP⁻¹ with D having diagonal entries 3 and -1, what is M²?
2. A matrix has only one independent eigenvector in 2D (like the shear from Chapter 7). What does that mean for diagonalizing it?
Key takeaway

M = PDP⁻¹ rewrites a transformation as: switch into the eigenvector basis (P⁻¹), scale independently along each axis (D), switch back (P). It only works when there are enough independent eigenvectors to form a full basis.

M = PDP⁻¹. P = eigenvector columns, D = eigenvalue diagonal. Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹.
