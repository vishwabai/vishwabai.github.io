Chapter 00 — Orientation

What robotics actually is, in one sentence

A robot is anything that senses the world, decides what to do about it, and acts to change the world — on its own, over and over, without a person doing each step by hand.

What you think robotics isBuilding a humanoid robot that walks and talks like a person — mostly mechanical engineering with some AI sprinkled on top.
What it actually isA robot can be a one-jointed arm welding the same spot a million times, or a vacuum with no arms at all. The "robotics" part isn't the body — it's the loop: measure, decide, move, repeat.

Here's the test for whether something is a robot: if you unplug the human and the loop keeps running on its own, sensing and reacting to a world that wasn't scripted in advance, it's a robot. A dishwasher runs a fixed timer — not a robot. A robot vacuum that detects a wall and turns — a robot, even with one cheap sensor and one motor.

Feynman analogy

Think of a robot like a person crossing a busy street

You look both ways (sense), your brain decides whether it's safe and picks a path (think), and your legs carry you across (act). You don't do this once — you keep glancing as you walk. That glance-decide-step cycle, running continuously, is the entire idea robotics is built on. Everything in this course — sensors, kinematics, control, planning — is just a more rigorous version of "look both ways."

Every chapter you're about to learn is a different specialization of the same central loop. Nothing here is unrelated trivia.

⚡ Common sticking point

People assume robotics is mostly about the body (motors, metal, 3D printing). In practice, most working roboticists spend the majority of their time on the think step — software that turns noisy sensor numbers into a decision. The hardware is often the easy 20%.

Key takeaway
  • A robot = a closed loop of sense → think → act, running without a human doing each cycle by hand.
  • The loop, not the body shape, is what defines "robot."
  • Every topic in this course is a deeper look at one part of that same loop.
Chapter 01 — Beginner

The robot's senses and muscles

Before a robot can think, it needs two things: a way to find out what's going on (a sensor), and a way to change what's going on (an actuator). Everything else in robotics is built on top of these two.

What you thinkSensors give a robot perfect, instant information — like a video game character that just "knows" its position.
What's actually trueEvery real sensor lies a little. A distance sensor might say "2.01m" when the true distance is 2.00m, and that error wobbles every reading. Robotics is largely the art of making good decisions from slightly-wrong numbers.
Feynman analogy 1

A sensor is a foggy window, not a clean photograph

Imagine reading a thermometer through frosted glass — you can tell roughly what it says, but never with perfect precision, and it jitters a little even when the temperature hasn't changed. That jitter is called noise, and no sensor is free of it.

Feynman analogy 2

An actuator is a muscle with a dial, not a switch

A light switch is on/off. A muscle can push gently or hard, fast or slow. Most actuators (motors) are the same — you don't just say "move," you say "push with this much force, for this long," and the real-world result still depends on friction, weight, and battery charge.

Top: a flat true value (dashed) vs. what the sensor reports (jittery). Bottom: a commanded step (dashed) vs. the actuator's real, lagged motion.

Reveal the why — what causes sensor noise and actuator lag?

Sensor noise comes from physics you can't avoid: electrical interference, tiny vibrations, quantization (a sensor that only reports whole millimeters can't tell you 2.4mm). Actuator lag comes from inertia — a motor and whatever it's attached to has mass, and mass resists sudden changes in speed. Both of these are why Chapter 5 (control) has to exist at all.

📐 Math deep dive — modeling noise and motor dynamics rigorously
The sensor noise model

Every real sensor reading z is modeled as the true value x plus a random error term ε:

z = x + ε,   ε ~ N(0, σ²)

ε is usually treated as Gaussian (bell-curve) noise with mean 0 and variance σ². σ (sigma) is the sensor's standard deviation — the datasheet number like "±2cm" is roughly 2σ. This single assumption is the foundation of nearly every filtering algorithm in Chapter 6: if you know σ, you know exactly how much to trust a given reading.

Averaging n independent readings of the same fixed quantity shrinks the noise:

σavg = σ / √n

This is why a robot that takes 100 LIDAR pings of a static wall gets a far tighter distance estimate than a robot trusting a single ping — the error shrinks with the square root of the sample count, not linearly, which is also why the *last* doubling of samples helps far less than the first.

The actuator's equation of motion

A DC motor driving a load obeys Newton's second law for rotation. Torque in, minus friction drag, equals inertia times angular acceleration:

J (dω/dt) = τ − bω
SymbolMeaning
Jmoment of inertia of the rotating load (resistance to angular acceleration)
ωangular velocity
τapplied torque, τ = Kt·I (proportional to motor current I)
bviscous friction / damping coefficient

Solving this differential equation for a step input torque gives an exponential approach to a final speed, with a characteristic time constant:

τmotor = J / b

This is precisely the "lag" you saw in the diagram above — a real motor can never jump instantly to a new speed because that would require infinite torque. The bigger J/b is, the more sluggish the response, and the more aggressively Chapter 5's controller has to compensate.

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why does no sensor give a robot a "perfect" reading, and what's one consequence of that?

Model answer: Every sensor is a physical device subject to electrical noise, vibration, and limited resolution, so its reading is always an estimate of the true value, not the true value itself. One consequence: a robot can't simply trust a single reading — it has to combine multiple readings over time (or multiple sensors) to get a more reliable picture, which is exactly what Chapter 6 (sensor fusion) covers.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • Sensors measure the world but always with some noise and delay.
  • Actuators change the world but never exactly as commanded — inertia and friction get in the way.
  • Good robotics software assumes both are imperfect from the start, rather than treating them as exact.
Chapter 02 — Beginner

Where am I? — reference frames

"The cup is 30cm in front of the gripper" only means something once you say whose front. Robotics solves this with reference frames: agreed-upon coordinate systems that every measurement is relative to.

What you thinkA robot has one single notion of "position" — like a dot on a map.
What's actually trueA robot usually juggles many positions at once: the camera's view of an object, the gripper's own joint position, the room's fixed coordinates. None of these numbers mean anything until you say which frame they're measured in — and a huge share of real robot bugs are "frame mismatch" bugs.
Feynman analogy 1

A reference frame is like "stage left" vs. "audience left"

An actor told to walk "left" means their own left, facing the audience — which is the audience's right. Theater people invented "stage left/right" specifically to avoid that mixup. Robots need the exact same discipline: every number must come with "measured from where, facing which way."

Feynman analogy 2

A transform is a translator between two languages

If the camera says "the cup is 10cm to my left," and you need to know where it is relative to the room's corner, you need a translator: a fixed rule for converting camera-language coordinates into room-language coordinates. That translator is called a transform, and it's nothing more than "shift then rotate."

The same target object has different coordinates in the world frame vs. the robot's own (rotated) frame. Use the slider below to rotate the robot frame and see how its read of "where the object is" changes — even though the object never moved.

Notice: the object's position in world coordinates never changes. Only the robot's rotated frame makes the numbers shift — which is exactly why mixing up frames causes a robot to "see" things in the wrong place.

⚡ Common sticking point

The order of rotate-then-translate vs. translate-then-rotate matters, and reversing it silently gives a wrong answer that still "looks like a number." This is the single most common bug category in real robot code — always be explicit about which frame a number lives in, and write it in variable names (e.g. cup_pos_in_camera_frame).

📐 Math deep dive — rotation matrices and homogeneous transforms
The 2D rotation matrix

Rotating a point by angle θ is a linear operation — multiplying by a 2×2 matrix:

⎡x'⎤   ⎡cos θ   −sin θ⎤ ⎡x⎤
⎣y'⎦  =  ⎣sin θ    cos θ⎦ ⎣y⎦

This is exactly the calculation the slider above runs on every drag: it rotates the object's world coordinates by −θ (the negative of the robot's heading) to find out where the object appears from the robot's point of view. Rotating "the world" backward by the robot's own heading is mathematically identical to rotating the robot's frame forward — that equivalence is worth sitting with, because it trips up almost everyone the first time.

Combining rotation and translation: the homogeneous transform

A frame can be both rotated and shifted relative to another. Robotics packages both operations into one 3×3 matrix (in 2D) called a homogeneous transform, by adding a constant "1" row:

⎡x'⎤   ⎡cos θ   −sin θ   tx⎤ ⎡x⎤
⎢y'⎥  =  ⎢sin θ    cos θ   ty⎥ ⎢y⎥
⎣1 ⎦   ⎣ 0       0       1 ⎦ ⎣1⎦

Call this matrix T. The payoff: chaining frames is now just matrix multiplication. If you know the transform from frame A to frame B, and from B to C, the transform straight from A to C is:

TA←C = TA←B · TB←C

Matrix multiplication is not commutative (T₁·T₂ ≠ T₂·T₁ in general) — which is the rigorous reason "rotate-then-translate" and "translate-then-rotate" give different answers. This single non-commutativity fact is responsible for a large fraction of all robotics coordinate bugs ever filed.

Why this scales to forward kinematics

Chapter 3 is nothing more than this same homogeneous transform, applied once per joint and multiplied together: Tbase→hand = T₁·T₂·T₃·...·Tn. Every transform in the chain just rotates by that joint's angle and translates by that link's length.

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why isn't "the object is at (3, 4)" a complete statement on its own?

Model answer: A coordinate pair like (3, 4) is only meaningful relative to some chosen origin and orientation — without saying which frame, you can't tell if that's 3 meters east of the room's corner or 3 meters in front of the robot's nose. Two different frames will report wildly different numbers for the exact same physical point, so the frame is part of the data, not an afterthought.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • A coordinate is meaningless without specifying its reference frame.
  • A transform converts a measurement from one frame's "language" into another's.
  • Frame-mismatch bugs are extremely common and usually silent — they don't crash, they just quietly point the robot at the wrong place.
Chapter 03 — Intermediate

Forward kinematics — where does the hand end up?

If you know every joint angle of a robot arm, can you calculate exactly where the fingertip is in space? Yes — and that calculation is called forward kinematics.

What you thinkThis requires advanced robotics-specific math you've never seen before.
What's actually trueIt's just the same "transform" idea from Chapter 2, chained link by link. If you can place one rotated frame relative to another, you can place a whole arm — you're just doing that step several times in a row.
Feynman analogy 1

An arm is a chain of "stand here, then walk this far"

Imagine giving directions: "From the flagpole, walk 10 paces, turn 30°, walk 6 more paces, turn 45°, walk 4 more." Each instruction only makes sense relative to where the previous instruction left you. A two-link robot arm is exactly this: shoulder angle places the elbow, elbow angle (relative to the upper arm) places the wrist.

Feynman analogy 2

It's like a human arm shadow puppet

Your shoulder and elbow angles fully determine where your fingertip is — you don't separately decide "fingertip position," it falls out automatically once both joint angles are fixed. Forward kinematics is just writing that automatic falling-out as an equation.

This is literally the forward kinematics equation: x = L₁sin(θ₁) + L₂sin(θ₁+θ₂), y = L₁cos(θ₁) + L₂cos(θ₁+θ₂) — every joint angle simply gets added into the chain.

⚡ Common sticking point

The elbow angle is usually measured relative to the upper arm, not relative to the world. Forgetting that and treating both angles as "absolute" is the single most common forward-kinematics bug — it's a frame mistake from Chapter 2 wearing a new costume.

📐 Math deep dive — the full FK chain, and the velocity Jacobian
Deriving the 2-link equation from Chapter 2's transform

Each link contributes one homogeneous transform: rotate by that joint's angle, then translate along the link length. Chaining two links (multiplying the two transforms from Chapter 2) and reading off the final x, y column gives exactly the equation used in the slider above:

x  =  L₁ sin θ₁  +  L₂ sin(θ₁ + θ₂)
y  =  L₁ cos θ₁  +  L₂ cos(θ₁ + θ₂)

Notice θ₂ always appears added to θ₁ — that's the algebraic fingerprint of "θ₂ is measured relative to link 1," exactly the sticking point above. Real industrial arms (5, 6, or 7 joints) use the identical idea with the formal bookkeeping system called Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) parameters: four numbers per joint (link length, link twist, link offset, joint angle) that fully define that joint's contribution to the chain, so the whole arm becomes one matrix product T = T₁(q₁)·T₂(q₂)·...·Tₙ(qₙ).

From position to velocity: the Jacobian

Forward kinematics tells you where the hand is. Often you also need how fast the hand is moving, given how fast each joint is spinning. The matrix that converts joint velocities into hand velocity is the Jacobian, built from partial derivatives:

⎡ẋ⎤   ⎡∂x/∂θ₁   ∂x/∂θ₂⎤ ⎡θ̇₁⎤
⎣ẏ⎦  =  ⎣∂y/∂θ₁   ∂y/∂θ₂⎦ ⎣θ̇₂⎦   =   J(θ) θ̇

For the 2-link arm, differentiating the position equations above gives:

∂x/∂θ₁ = L₁cos θ₁ + L₂cos(θ₁+θ₂)    ∂x/∂θ₂ = L₂cos(θ₁+θ₂)
∂y/∂θ₁ = −L₁sin θ₁ − L₂sin(θ₁+θ₂)    ∂y/∂θ₂ = −L₂sin(θ₁+θ₂)

The Jacobian reappears constantly in robotics: it's used to convert force at the hand into torque at each joint (its transpose), and a Jacobian that loses rank — drops from full rank to a lower one — flags a singularity, a pose where the arm momentarily loses the ability to move in some direction no matter how the joints spin (the classic example: a fully outstretched arm can't push further outward).

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why is forward kinematics "easy" — what makes it a straightforward calculation rather than a search?

Model answer: Forward kinematics goes in the "easy" direction: angles → position. There's exactly one answer for any given set of joint angles, computed by chaining transforms link by link, with no guessing involved. It only becomes hard (Chapter 4) when you flip the question around and ask "what angles get me to a chosen position," because many different angle combinations can sometimes reach the same point.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • Forward kinematics: joint angles → end-effector position. Always exactly one answer.
  • It's the same "chain of transforms" idea from Chapter 2, applied link by link.
  • Joint angles are usually relative to the previous link, not the world — track your frames.
Chapter 04 — Intermediate

Inverse kinematics — working backward

Now flip the question: you want the fingertip at a specific point — what joint angles get you there? This is inverse kinematics, and it's harder because the answer isn't always unique.

What you thinkIf forward kinematics has one clean equation, inverse kinematics must too — just solve the equation "backward."
What's actually trueFor many arm shapes, a single target point can be reached by more than one combination of joint angles (think: elbow up vs. elbow down). Sometimes there are infinite solutions, sometimes zero (the point is out of reach). Inverse kinematics is a search-and-choose problem, not a single plug-in-the-numbers formula.
Feynman analogy 1

It's like reaching for a doorknob with your own arm

Try it: reach for a doorknob with your elbow pointing down, then again with your elbow pointing up and out to the side. Your hand lands on the exact same doorknob both times — two completely different joint configurations, one target. Your nervous system picks one without you noticing; a robot has to pick deliberately.

Feynman analogy 2

It's like finding a route on a map vs. checking where a route leads

Checking "if I drive this exact sequence of turns, where do I end up?" (forward) is a quick calculation. "What sequence of turns gets me to this address?" (inverse) usually needs a search across many possible routes, because more than one route can work.

⚡ Common sticking point

Newcomers expect IK to always have an answer. In reality, every IK solver needs to handle three outcomes: no solution (unreachable), one solution, and multiple solutions (need a rule for picking, e.g. "closest to current pose" to avoid sudden jerky jumps).

📐 Math deep dive — solving IK with the law of cosines
Setting up the triangle

The base, elbow, and target point form a triangle with known side lengths L₁ and L₂, and a third side equal to the straight-line distance from base to target:

r = √(x² + y²)

For a solution to exist at all, this triangle must be physically possible — the target can't be farther than the arm's full reach, nor closer than the arm's minimum fold:

|L₁ − L₂| ≤ r ≤ L₁ + L₂

That inequality is exactly what the sandbox above checks before declaring a target "unreachable."

Solving the elbow angle

The law of cosines relates all three sides of the triangle to the interior angle at the elbow:

r² = L₁² + L₂² − 2L₁L₂ cos(π − θ₂)

Rearranged to solve directly for the elbow joint angle θ₂:

θ₂ = ±cos−1 ⎛x² + y² − L₁² − L₂²⁄2L₁L₂⎞

The ± is the entire reason two solutions exist: +θ₂ folds the elbow one way, −θ₂ folds it the other, and both land the hand at the identical (x, y) — algebra's confirmation of "elbow up vs. elbow down."

Solving the shoulder angle

Once θ₂ is fixed, θ₁ is the angle to the target minus a correction for how much the elbow bend "steals" from that direction:

θ₁ = atan2(x, y) − atan2(L₂ sin θ₂,  L₁ + L₂ cos θ₂)

atan2 (rather than plain atan) is used throughout robotics because it correctly resolves the angle in all four quadrants, including straight up, down, left, and right — a plain arctangent silently breaks at those exact boundary cases.

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why can the same target point have more than one valid set of joint angles?

Model answer: A target point only fixes the position of the very last link. The earlier joints have freedom to bend in different ways (e.g. elbow up vs. elbow down) while the chain of transforms still lands the final link at the same coordinates — geometry allows more than one chain shape to reach an identical endpoint.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • Inverse kinematics: target position → joint angles. Can have zero, one, or many answers.
  • A real IK solver must detect unreachable targets and choose among multiple valid solutions.
  • "Closest to the current pose" is a common tie-breaker to keep motion smooth rather than jumpy.
Chapter 05 — Intermediate

Closing the loop — PID control

Knowing the right joint angles (Chapter 3 & 4) doesn't mean the motor gets there cleanly. Control is the science of correcting course in real time, the same way you'd nudge a steering wheel to stay in your lane.

What you thinkTo move a motor to a target, you just tell it "go to 90°" and it goes.
What's actually trueA motor that's simply told "go to 90°" will typically overshoot, oscillate, or stall short, because of inertia and friction (Chapter 1). You need continuous feedback: measure the error, correct, measure again — many times per second.
Feynman analogy 1

It's like adjusting shower temperature

You don't set the shower knob once and walk away — you feel the water, it's too cold, you turn it up a bit, wait, feel again, maybe overshoot to too hot, nudge back. That iterative "feel, adjust, feel, adjust" is literally what a control loop does to a motor, just many times per second instead of every few seconds.

Feynman analogy 2

P, I, and D are three different "instincts" combined

Proportional: "the further off I am, the harder I correct" (like steering harder the further you drift from your lane). Integral: "I've been a little off for a while now, time to correct that lingering bias" (like noticing you've been slightly biased left for the last mile and compensating). Derivative: "I'm closing in fast, ease off before I overshoot" (like braking earlier when approaching a stop sign quickly).

The loop never stops: measure → compare to target → correct → the world changes → measure again. Hundreds of times per second on a real robot.

⚡ Common sticking point

Beginners crank up P to "make it move faster" and get violent oscillation instead, because high P alone has nothing to stop it from overshooting. The fix isn't more P — it's adding D to anticipate and brake, and a touch of I only if there's a persistent steady-state offset.

📐 Math deep dive — the PID equation, discretized, and how to tune it
The continuous-time control law

The output sent to the motor is the weighted sum of three terms, all computed from the same error signal e(t) = target − measured:

u(t) = Kpe(t) + Ki∫₀ᵗ e(τ)dτ + Kd de(t)/dt

The integral term accumulates every bit of past error — it's literally "the area under the error curve so far" — which is exactly why a small but persistent error eventually triggers a real correction, even one too small for the proportional term to notice on its own.

What a real microcontroller actually runs

A robot's processor doesn't do calculus — it runs in discrete time steps of size Δt, so the integral becomes a running sum and the derivative becomes a simple difference:

uk = Kpek + KiΔt Σᵢ₌₀k ei + Kd (ek − ek−1)/Δt

This is the exact formula the live playground above evaluates, once per simulated tick. It's also worth knowing that the unbounded sum Σei can grow forever if the target is ever unreachable (a jammed motor, say) — a failure mode called integral windup, fixed in practice by clamping the running sum to a maximum size.

A standard starting recipe: Ziegler–Nichols tuning

Rather than guessing P, I, D from scratch, a classic method is to first set I = D = 0, raise P until the system oscillates at a steady, unshrinking amplitude — call that gain Ku (the "ultimate gain") and the oscillation period Tu — then apply this table:

ControllerKpKiKd
P only0.50 Ku——
PI0.45 Ku1.2 Kp/Tu—
PID0.60 Ku2 Kp/TuKpTu/8

It's a starting point, not a final answer — real systems still get fine-tuned by hand afterward, exactly the way you've been doing with the sliders above.

Why it oscillates: the frequency-domain view

Taking the Laplace transform of the control law turns the calculus into algebra, giving the controller's transfer function:

C(s) = U(s)/E(s) = Kp + Ki/s + Kds

Combined with the motor's own transfer function from Chapter 1's J(dω/dt) = τ − bω, this fraction's denominator — once factored — tells you, before ever running the robot, whether a given P/I/D choice produces a stable, decaying response or a runaway oscillation. This is the formal machinery underneath the trial-and-error you just did with the sliders.

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why does a robot need to "measure again" continuously instead of moving once and stopping?

Model answer: Because actuators don't move exactly as commanded (Chapter 1) — friction, weight, and momentum throw off a single open-loop command. By repeatedly comparing the actual measured position to the target and correcting, the robot can cancel out those real-world disturbances as they happen, rather than hoping a single command lands perfectly.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • Control = continuously measuring error and correcting, not issuing a single command.
  • P reacts to current error, I reacts to lingering error, D reacts to the rate of change to prevent overshoot.
  • Too much P alone causes oscillation; D is usually the fix, not less P.
Chapter 06 — Advanced

Perception & SLAM — building a map while lost in it

A delivery robot in a building it has never seen has to solve two problems at once: "where am I?" and "what does this place look like?" Solving both together is called SLAM — Simultaneous Localization and Mapping.

What you thinkYou'd obviously map the building first, then have the robot find its position on that finished map.
What's actually trueYou usually can't do that — there often is no pre-made map, and even GPS is unreliable indoors. The robot has to build the map and locate itself on that same map at the same time, using its own imperfect motion estimates and imperfect sensor readings to bootstrap both.
Feynman analogy 1

It's like exploring a dark house with a flashlight, remembering as you go

You don't have a floor plan. You walk forward, the flashlight reveals a wall here, a doorway there, and you mentally sketch the rooms as you pass through them — while also keeping track of how far you've walked from the front door. Get the step-count slightly wrong and your mental sketch slowly drifts out of alignment with reality.

Feynman analogy 2

Sensor fusion is like trusting two different witnesses

If one friend says "we walked about 10 steps" and another says "I saw a red door pass on our left," neither account alone tells you exactly where you are — but combined, your confidence in the true position goes up. SLAM combines many such "witnesses" (wheel odometry, LIDAR scans, camera frames) the same way.

Left: scattered, low-confidence readings right after entering an unknown room. Right: the same space after SLAM has fused dozens of scans into a confident map with the robot located inside it.

🃏 Flashcards — perception vocabulary
⚡ Common sticking point

People assume more sensors always means a better map. In reality, the hard part of SLAM is loop closure — recognizing "I've been in this exact spot before" after wandering away and coming back, so accumulated drift can be corrected. Without loop closure, even great sensors slowly produce a map that curls in on itself.

📐 Math deep dive — the Bayes filter and the Kalman filter
The Bayes filter: the master equation behind SLAM

A robot's confidence about its own state x at time t — its belief — is updated in two stages every cycle: predict using the motion model, then correct using the sensor model.

Predict:  bel̄(xt) = ∫ P(xt | xt−1, ut) bel(xt−1) dxt−1
Update:   bel(xt) = η P(zt | xt) bel̄(xt)

In plain language: "predict" spreads out your confidence using how the robot just moved (and how unreliable that motion estimate is), then "update" sharpens it back up using how well a new sensor reading matches each candidate position. η is just a normalizing constant so the probabilities sum to 1. Every SLAM algorithm in production — particle filters, Kalman filters, pose-graph optimizers — is a different, more computationally tractable way of approximating this exact equation.

The Kalman filter: the Bayes filter for straight lines and Gaussians

When the motion and sensor models are linear and all the noise is Gaussian, the Bayes filter integral above collapses into simple matrix algebra — the Kalman filter. Predict step:

x̂t− = A x̂t−1 + B ut
Pt− = A Pt−1 AT + Q

x̂ is the estimated state (e.g. position and velocity), P is the uncertainty (covariance) around that estimate, A models how the state evolves on its own, B·u is the effect of a known control input, and Q is how much noisy uncertainty the motion itself adds each step. Update step, once a new measurement z arrives:

Kt = Pt− HT (H Pt− HT + R)−1
x̂t = x̂t− + Kt(zt − H x̂t−)
Pt = (I − KtH) Pt−

Kt is the Kalman gain — the precise, principled answer to "how much should I trust this new reading versus my existing estimate?" When the sensor is very noisy (R is large), K shrinks toward 0 and the filter barely moves its estimate; when the sensor is very precise (R is small), K grows toward 1 and the filter snaps almost entirely to the new reading. That single ratio is the rigorous version of the "trusting two witnesses" analogy from earlier in this chapter.

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why can't a robot just build the map first and then localize itself on it, the way you might assume?

Model answer: Building an accurate map requires knowing where you were standing for each sensor reading, but figuring out where you're standing usually requires already having a map to compare against — the two problems depend on each other. SLAM algorithms solve this by estimating both simultaneously and letting each one improve the other over time, rather than insisting on a strict order.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • SLAM solves "where am I" and "what's the map" together, because each depends on the other.
  • Sensor fusion combines multiple imperfect "witnesses" into a more confident estimate.
  • Loop closure — recognizing a previously visited place — is what keeps long-term maps from drifting.
Chapter 07 — Expert / Synthesis

Planning & behavior — deciding what to do

With a map (Ch. 6), a way to move precisely (Ch. 3–5), the last piece is deciding what to do: which path to take, and which behavior to run when the unexpected happens.

What you think"Path planning" means finding the single best route, like a GPS app, and that's the whole problem.
What's actually truePath planning is necessary but not sufficient. A real robot also needs a behavior layer above it deciding things like "should I be navigating at all right now, or should I be waiting, charging, or asking for help?" Planning is nested: high-level behavior choice, then a path for that behavior, then moment-to-moment control to follow it.
Feynman analogy 1

It's like a delivery driver's whole day, not just one route

A delivery driver doesn't just compute the shortest path to one address — they decide the order of stops, react if a road is closed, and know when to simply stop driving and ask for directions. The route-finding (path planning) is one layer inside a bigger decision-making process (behavior).

Feynman analogy 2

A behavior tree is like a flowchart of habits, not a single decision

Think of your own morning routine as a tree of fallbacks: "if the alarm didn't go off, check phone; if phone's dead, ask a family member; if no one's around, just guess the time." A robot's behavior layer is built the same way — a tree of "try this, and if it fails, fall back to that."

Three nested layers, slow-to-fast: behavior decides the goal, planning finds a route, control executes it tick by tick.

⚡ Common sticking point

Students often plan a path once and assume it's final. Real environments change — a person walks into the hallway — so planning must be cheap enough to re-run frequently. Spending too much compute on a "perfect" path that's stale by the time it finishes executing is a classic mistake.

📐 Math deep dive — A* search and configuration space
Path planning as graph search

Once the map is divided into cells (Chapter 6's occupancy grid), finding a route becomes a graph search problem: each free cell is a node, each move to a neighboring free cell is an edge. The most widely used algorithm, A* (A-star), picks which node to expand next by minimizing:

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
SymbolMeaning
g(n)the actual cost already paid to reach node n from the start (known exactly)
h(n)a heuristic — an estimate of the remaining cost from n to the goal

A common, cheap heuristic is straight-line (Euclidean) distance to the goal, since no real path can ever be shorter than a straight line:

h(n) = √((xn − xgoal)² + (yn − ygoal)²)

A* is guaranteed to find the shortest possible path as long as h(n) never overestimates the true remaining cost — a property called admissibility. Set h(n) = 0 everywhere and A* reduces exactly to Dijkstra's algorithm, which explores uniformly outward in all directions; a good admissible heuristic just lets A* skip exploring directions that obviously lead away from the goal, which is why A* is normally dramatically faster in practice.

Configuration space: planning for a body, not a point

A* plans for a single point moving between grid cells, but a real robot has a physical size and possibly multiple joint angles. Robotics solves this by planning in configuration space (C-space) — a space where each axis is one degree of freedom of the robot (e.g. x, y, heading for a mobile robot; θ₁, θ₂ for the 2-link arm), and obstacles are inflated outward by the robot's own radius before search ever begins:

Cobstacle = { q ∈ C | robot(q) ∩ obstacle ≠ ∅ }

Once obstacles are expressed this way, the robot can be treated as a single point inside C-space for the purposes of search — the same A* algorithm above runs unmodified, just in a space with more axes (for the 2-link arm, C-space is literally the 2D θ₁–θ₂ plane from Chapter 4's sandbox, with unreachable or colliding angle combinations marked as obstacles).

✍️ Explain it back

In your own words: why is path planning "necessary but not sufficient" for a robot to act well in the world?

Model answer: A path tells the robot how to get from A to B, but doesn't decide whether going to B is even the right thing to do right now, or what to do if the path becomes blocked. A behavior layer above planning makes those higher-level calls — when to plan, when to pause, when to ask for help — so planning is just one tool the behavior layer reaches for.
✅ Self-check
Key takeaway
  • Behavior (what to do) sits above path planning (which route) which sits above control (how to follow it).
  • Re-planning needs to be cheap and frequent, because real environments change mid-execution.
  • "Robust" robotics is mostly about graceful fallback, not a single perfect plan.

Where to go next

These four are the standard next steps roboticists actually take after learning the fundamentals — one framework to build with, two books to go deeper on the math, and hands-on practice to feel where equations and reality diverge.

🧩

ROS / ROS2

Software framework

The middleware that wires everything in this course together on a real robot. Sensor drivers, the SLAM stack, the planner, and the controller each run as separate nodes that talk over publish/subscribe topics — so a camera node can publish images that a perception node subscribes to, without either knowing the other exists.

You'll use: rviz (visualize frames, maps, and sensor data live), Nav2 (a ready-made planning + control stack built on Ch. 5–7), MoveIt (inverse kinematics and motion planning for arms, built on Ch. 3–4), and tf2 (manages exactly the reference-frame transforms from Chapter 2 across a whole robot automatically).

Probabilistic Robotics

Thrun, Burgard & Fox

The standard graduate text for everything in Chapter 6, taken to full rigor: Bayes filters derived from first principles, the Kalman filter and its nonlinear cousins (Extended and Unscented KF), particle filters for Monte Carlo localization, and occupancy-grid and pose-graph SLAM.

Read this if: the Bayes filter and Kalman filter equations in Chapter 6's math deep dive made sense but you want the full derivations, convergence proofs, and worked robot examples behind them.

Modern Robotics

Lynch & Park — free online

A rigorous, freely available textbook (with matching video lectures and code libraries in Python/MATLAB/Mathematica) covering kinematics and dynamics properly: screw theory and the product-of-exponentials formulation, a more elegant and singularity-robust alternative to the Denavit–Hartenberg parameters mentioned in Chapter 3, plus trajectory generation, dynamics, and motion planning.

Read this if: Chapters 3–4's two-link arm math felt approachable and you want the general n-joint, 3D version with full derivations.

Simulators & hardware

Where the equations meet reality

Gazebo and Webots are free, physics-accurate simulators that integrate with ROS — you can run every algorithm in this course against a simulated robot with realistic sensor noise and motor dynamics before ever touching hardware. For a photorealistic, GPU-accelerated option aimed at perception and reinforcement learning, look at NVIDIA Isaac Sim.

Then go physical: a low-cost two-wheeled kit (e.g. a TurtleBot, or a DIY Raspberry Pi/Arduino rig with an ultrasonic or LIDAR sensor) is the fastest way to feel firsthand what Chapter 1 meant by "no sensor gives a perfect reading" — simulators can approximate noise, but real hardware guarantees it.
