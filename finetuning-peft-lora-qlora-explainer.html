A VISUAL EXPLAINER
Every dial below is one learned number inside a language model. There are billions of them. This deck is about which ones we actually need to touch.
THE BIG PICTURE

A language model is just... numbers

Every layer is a giant matrix of weights. Each cell is one number the model learned by reading text. "Training" means nudging these numbers.

Think of the whole model as one enormous control panel. Fine-tuning is the act of turning some of those dials.
THE BIG PICTURE

Training = nudging knobs downhill

Predict → compare to the right answer → measure the error ("loss") → nudge every weight a little in the direction that lowers it. Repeat billions of times.

Do this across trillions of words and you get a pretrained model. Fine-tuning is exactly this same downhill walk — just on your new, smaller dataset.
THE MATH

One weight update, spelled out

Every one of those "nudges" on the last slide is the same three-line recipe, repeated for every parameter.

Full fine-tuning runs this exact line for all 7 billion θᵢ, every single step. LoRA will only ever run it for the numbers inside B and A.
FULL FINE-TUNING

The obvious approach: move every knob

Backprop computes a gradient for every parameter. The optimizer updates all of them, every step, until the model fits your new task.

It works — but every one of those 7 billion numbers now needs a gradient, an optimizer state, and a copy in memory. That's expensive, as we're about to see.
THE MATH

Where do 7 billion numbers come from?

Add up the matrices in one transformer block, then multiply by the number of blocks stacked on top of each other.

Every one of those matrices is a candidate for fine-tuning. Full fine-tuning touches all of them — LoRA will touch a sliver of each.
THE COST OF "MOVE EVERYTHING"

Every trainable number costs 4×

Weights, gradients, and Adam's two optimizer states all live in GPU memory at once. A 7B model doesn't cost 7B numbers of memory — it costs closer to 4–8× that.

≈ 84 GB just to fine-tune a 7B model — before you've even loaded a batch of data. It doesn't fit on a single GPU.
THE MATH

Why Adam costs 2 extra numbers per weight

Instead of just using today's gradient, Adam remembers a running average of gradients — and of their squares — for every single parameter.

Freeze a weight, and Adam never needs m or v for it either. That's the other half of why PEFT saves so much memory.
THE KEY INSIGHT

Do we need to move every knob?

Researchers measured the actual weight changes during fine-tuning and found something surprising: almost all of the useful signal lives in a tiny handful of directions.

This is the idea LoRA is built on: an update matrix ΔW can carry almost all its meaning through a low-rank approximation — a handful of directions instead of millions of independent numbers.
THE MATH

What does "rank" actually mean?

A rank-1 matrix is nothing exotic — it's just one column of numbers times one row of numbers. Every entry is a product of the two.

This is the entire mathematical basis of LoRA: any matrix, including a fine-tuning update, can be approximated as a sum of a handful of rank-1 pieces.
PEFT

Freeze the giant. Train a patch.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning locks the pretrained weights in place and adds a small set of brand-new, trainable parameters next to them.

There are several PEFT recipes — adapters, prefix-tuning, prompt-tuning. The one that won on quality and simplicity is called LoRA. Let's open it up.
LoRA

LoRA's move: ΔW ≈ B × A

Instead of learning a full, huge update matrix, LoRA learns two skinny matrices whose product approximates it. One tall and thin, one short and wide.

Only B and A get trained. ΔW is never stored or computed directly — it only ever exists as this product, on the fly.
LoRA MECHANICS

A careful start: B = 0, A = random

At step zero, B is filled with zeros and A with small random numbers. So ΔW = B·A is exactly zero — training starts from the untouched pretrained model.

As training proceeds, B's zeros slowly wake up. The adapter's influence grows smoothly from nothing — no jarring jump at the start of fine-tuning.
LoRA MECHANICS

Where B and A actually live

B contributes one thin strip of columns, A one thin strip of rows. Multiplied together, they reconstruct a full-size update — without ever storing the full grid.

Multiply that thin column-strip by that thin row-strip and, entry by entry, you can reconstruct an approximation of the whole update matrix. That's the entire trick.
LoRA MECHANICS

The scaling knob: α ⁄ r

LoRA doesn't add B·A directly — it adds (α⁄r)·B·A. That ratio keeps the adapter's effective strength stable no matter what rank you pick.

This is why you can raise the rank to give the adapter more capacity without having to re-tune the learning rate from scratch.
LoRA MECHANICS

The payoff, in real numbers

Take one realistic 4096 × 4096 weight matrix — a large model has hundreds of these. Compare the full update to a rank-8 LoRA update.

16,777,216 → 65,536. Do this across every attention matrix in the model and full fine-tuning's 7B trainable parameters can shrink to a few million — sometimes less than 1%.
LoRA IN PRACTICE

Which weights actually get an adapter?

Every transformer block has six obvious candidates. LoRA can be attached to any subset of them.

The original LoRA paper found adapting just Q and V often close to matches full fine-tuning. QLoRA setups often go further and adapt all of them.
LoRA IN PRACTICE

Choosing rank r: diminishing returns

More rank means more capacity to match full fine-tuning — but the gains flatten out fast. Most tasks are happy in the 4–64 range.

Past a certain point, extra rank mostly adds trainable parameters without adding accuracy — you're back to paying full fine-tuning's bill for little gain.
HOW LoRA RUNS

Forward pass: two paths, one sum

h = Wx + BAx. The input flows through the frozen original weights and through the tiny LoRA bypass. The two outputs are simply added.

Notice the bypass path is tiny — just two small matrix multiplies. It barely adds any compute or latency to the original model.
HOW LoRA TRAINS

Backward pass: gradients skip W entirely

The optimizer only ever touches A and B. W never receives a gradient, never gets an optimizer state, never moves.

This is the whole memory win: no gradient for 7 billion frozen weights, no Adam moments for them either. Only A and B need that expensive bookkeeping.
AFTER TRAINING

Merge B×A back in. Pay nothing at inference.

Once training is done, fold the update directly into the original weights: W′ = W + (α⁄r)·BA. No separate path left to run — just one ordinary matrix again.

This is why LoRA adapters are so portable: keep W untouched, ship a tiny B/A file, and merge on demand — or swap adapters entirely for a different task.
QLoRA

QLoRA: shrink the frozen giant too

LoRA already freezes W. So why store it at full precision? QLoRA packs the frozen base into 4-bit numbers (NF4), while B and A stay in higher precision for stable training.

A 7B model's frozen base drops from roughly 14 GB to ~3.5 GB. Combined with LoRA's tiny trainable slice, you can fine-tune a 7B (even 65B) model on a single consumer GPU.
QLoRA

Why NF4, not plain 4-bit integers?

Pretrained weights cluster tightly around zero, roughly bell-shaped. NF4 places its 16 buckets where the data actually lives — not spread evenly.

Same 4 bits per weight, meaningfully less rounding error — because the "resolution" is spent exactly where the weight values are dense.
QLoRA

Two more tricks in QLoRA's bag

Compress the compression itself. And let optimizer states spill to CPU memory when the GPU is full — the same idea your OS uses to page RAM to disk.

Neither trick changes the math you've already learned — they're just careful bookkeeping so training doesn't crash on real hardware.
QLoRA

Double quantization, by the numbers

Every block of weights needs its own scale constant to dequantize correctly. Store enough of those, and even they start to add up.

None of this touches accuracy — it's pure bookkeeping. But at model scale, "small" overheads compound fast.
QLoRA

One formula, the whole method

Everything from the last several slides collapses into a single forward-pass equation.

A frozen, heavily compressed giant — plus a small, high-precision patch that does all the learning. That's the entire idea, start to finish.
SIDE BY SIDE

The scoreboard, same 7B model

Full fine-tuning, LoRA, and QLoRA — trainable parameters and peak memory, compared directly.

On most tasks LoRA and QLoRA land within a whisker of full fine-tuning quality — at a fraction of the parameters and memory. That trade is why they became the default.
WHERE IT'S GOING

Beyond LoRA, in one breath

The core idea — freeze the giant, train something small — keeps getting refined.

Different tweaks to the same trick. The underlying bet — that fine-tuning updates are low-rank — is the one idea that ties them all together.
RECAP

Say it simply, or you don't understand it yet

Four ideas, one sentence each.

Same recipe every time: freeze what already works, train the smallest thing that can still learn the new trick.
