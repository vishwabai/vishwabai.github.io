Chapter 1

Data Itself

Before you can summarize, test, or predict anything, you have to know what kind of thing you're holding. A spreadsheet column isn't just "numbers" — it's a specific kind of measurement, and that type decides every tool you're allowed to use on it.

What you think

"Data" is just numbers in a column. If it's a number, I can average it, plot it, and run any statistic on it.

What's actually true

A customer ID is a number, but averaging it is meaningless. Data has a type — categorical, ordinal, or numeric — and the type, not the fact that it's stored as a digit, decides what operations are valid.

🧰

The toolbox analogy

A wrench and a screwdriver both look like "metal tools," but you can't use a wrench on a Phillips screw. Data types work the same way: mean, median, and mode are different tools, and only some fit each type of data.

🎽

The race-bib analogy

Runner #7 didn't finish "7 times better" than runner #1 — the number is just a label. T-shirt sizes (S/M/L) have order but no fixed gap between them. Height in cm has both order and a true, measurable distance. Three different jobs for three different number-shapes.

Three branches a single data column can grow into — each unlocking different operations.

Reveal the why — why does it matter so much?

If you average a "ranking" (1st, 2nd, 3rd place) you get nonsense like "1.5th place" — a category that doesn't exist. If you compute "mode" (most frequent value) on someone's exact height in cm, you'll usually get one value that happens to repeat by coincidence, telling you nothing about the "typical" height. The type of data is a contract: it tells you which questions are even askable.

📐 The Math — frequencies, proportions, and the mode

Even "just labels" support real arithmetic — you count them. For a categorical column with categories c₁, c₂, …, cₖ, the frequency of category cᵢ is simply how many rows equal it, and the proportion is that count divided by the total:

proportion(cᵢ) = count(cᵢ) / n     where n = total rows

The mode — the only "center" measure that's valid for categorical data — is just the category with the highest count:

mode = argmax cᵢ  [ count(cᵢ) ]
Worked example

Browser column from 20 requests: Chrome ×12, Firefox ×5, Safari ×3.

  1. proportion(Chrome) = 12/20 = 0.60 → 60%
  2. proportion(Firefox) = 5/20 = 0.25 → 25%
  3. proportion(Safari) = 3/20 = 0.15 → 15%
mode = Chrome (the highest count, 12)
⚡ Common sticking point

People get tripped up by numbers that look numeric but aren't — ZIP codes, ID numbers, phone numbers, even "rating out of 5" stars. The test that actually works: ask "does subtracting two of these values produce a meaningful distance?" 90210 − 10001 means nothing. 5 stars − 2 stars arguably doesn't mean a fixed "3 units of happiness" either (that's why star ratings are ordinal, not numeric, even though everyone averages them anyway).

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

T-shirt size has an order (S < M < L) but the "distance" between sizes isn't a fixed, meaningful number — there's no guarantee L − M equals M − S in any real unit. Averaging requires that equal numeric gaps mean the same thing everywhere on the scale, which ordinal data doesn't promise. So "the average size was 2.4" is a number with no real-world referent.

Before computing anything, name the type of your column: categorical (labels), ordinal (ordered labels), or numeric (true distances). The type — not the fact it's stored as digits — decides which statistics are valid.

Chapter 2

Center & Spread

"The average user session is 4.2 minutes" sounds like a fact. It's actually a compression — you took a thousand numbers and threw away everything except one. The question is what that one number hides, and how to choose a better one.

What you think

The "average" (mean) is always the fair, neutral way to describe a typical value.

What's actually true

The mean is dragged hard by outliers. If 9 engineers earn ₹12L and one earns ₹2Cr, the "average salary" is wildly misleading — the median (the middle value) describes the typical engineer far better.

⚖️

The see-saw analogy

The mean is the exact balancing point of a see-saw with each data point as a weight. One person sitting very far out (an outlier) drags the whole balance point toward them — even if everyone else is clustered near the middle.

📏

The rubber-band analogy

Standard deviation is "how far the rubber band stretches on average" — it measures typical distance from the center. A tight cluster has a short stretch (low spread); scattered points stretch the band a long way (high spread).

Reveal the why — why is the median so stubborn?

The median only cares about position — "what's the middle value when sorted" — not magnitude. Whether the highest earner makes ₹50L or ₹50Cr, they're still just "the last one in line," so the median doesn't move. The mean cares about magnitude directly because it sums every value, so one huge number drags the total (and thus the average) upward.

📐 The Math — variance, std. deviation, and the n−1 correction

The mean is the balancing point you already know:

x̄ = (1/n) Σᵢ xᵢ

Variance averages the squared distance of every point from that mean (squaring makes negative and positive distances both count, and penalizes large deviations more):

σ² = (1/n) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)²

Standard deviation is just the square root of variance, which brings the units back to the original scale (e.g. ₹, not ₹²):

σ = √σ²

One subtlety: when you only have a sample (not the full population) and want to estimate the population's variance, dividing by n slightly underestimates the true spread. The fix — Bessel's correction — divides by n−1 instead:

s² = (1/(n−1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)²   (sample variance)
Worked example

Five response times (ms): 10, 12, 14, 16, 18.

  1. Mean: (10+12+14+16+18)/5 = 70/5 = 14
  2. Squared deviations: (−4)²=16, (−2)²=4, 0²=0, 2²=4, 4²=16 → sum = 40
  3. Sample variance: 40 / (5−1) = 40/4 = 10
Sample standard deviation s = √10 ≈ 3.16 ms
⚡ Common sticking point

People assume "spread" just means "the range" (max − min). Range is wrecked by a single extreme point, just like the mean is. Standard deviation is more robust because it looks at every point's typical distance from the center, not just the two most extreme ones — but it's still pulled (less violently) by outliers, which is why for skewed data people often report median + IQR (interquartile range) instead.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

Median salary isn't distorted by a handful of very high earners (executives, founders), so it represents what a "typical" employee actually takes home. Mean salary gets pulled upward by those few high values, making the company look like it pays more generously than most employees actually experience.

Key takeaway

Mean, median, and mode all answer "what's typical?" — but they disagree under skew. Always pair a center number with a spread number (std. dev. or IQR); a center alone hides whether your data is tight or wild.

Chapter 3

Probability Basics

Probability is just a number between 0 and 1 that says "how often would this happen if we could rewind reality and replay it many times." Almost every mistake beginners make comes from forgetting to ask: probability of what, given what?

What you think

If a coin has landed heads 4 times in a row, it's "due" for tails — the universe will balance it out.

What's actually true

The coin has no memory. Each flip is independent: the probability of tails on flip 5 is still exactly 50%, regardless of the last four outcomes. This is the gambler's fallacy, and it fools even careful engineers when they look at A/B test streaks.

🎯

The dartboard analogy

Probability is the fraction of the dartboard's area a region covers. "P(bullseye) = 0.05" just means: if you threw a thousand random darts, about 50 would land in that tiny circle. It's a statement about long-run frequency, not about any single throw.

🔍

The "given" lens analogy

Conditional probability — P(A | B) — is like looking at the dartboard through a window that only shows region B. You're not asking "what fraction of the whole board is A," you're asking "what fraction of just this window is A." Zooming into a subset changes the apparent odds.

Hover a region above…

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) — "of the engineers who know SQL, what fraction also know Python?"

Reveal the why — why does conditioning change the probability?

Conditioning shrinks your universe. Instead of asking "what fraction of ALL engineers know Python," P(A|B) asks "of the smaller group who already know SQL, what fraction also know Python." If SQL-knowers are disproportionately backend engineers who also tend to know Python, P(A|B) will be higher than the plain P(A). The denominator changed, so the fraction changed — even though nothing about Python itself changed.

📐 The Math — the addition rule and Bayes' theorem

For two events that can overlap, simply adding their probabilities double-counts the overlap, so you have to subtract it back out (the addition rule):

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

The conditional probability rule from this chapter can be rearranged into one of the most useful formulas in all of statistics — Bayes' theorem — which lets you flip a conditional probability around:

P(A | B) = [ P(B | A) × P(A) ] / P(B)
Worked example — the classic test-accuracy trap

A disease affects 1% of a population. A test for it is 95% accurate (correctly flags 95% of sick people, and correctly clears 95% of healthy people). You test positive. What's the chance you're actually sick?

  1. P(Sick) = 0.01, P(Positive | Sick) = 0.95
  2. P(Positive | Healthy) = 0.05 (the 5% false-positive rate)
  3. P(Positive) = 0.95×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059
  4. P(Sick | Positive) = (0.95 × 0.01) / 0.059 = 0.0095 / 0.059
P(Sick | Positive) ≈ 16% — far lower than the test's "95% accurate" headline suggests, because the disease is rare
⚡ Common sticking point

"Independent" and "mutually exclusive" get confused constantly. Independent means knowing one event tells you nothing about the other (rolling a 6 on die A says nothing about die B). Mutually exclusive means the two events can never happen together (rolling a 6 and rolling a 3 on the same single die). Independent events usually can co-occur; mutually exclusive events by definition cannot.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

Each flip is a fresh, independent physical event — the coin's mass and the way it's flipped don't change because of past outcomes. The probability of heads is fixed by the physics of the flip itself, not by some abstract "fairness debt" the universe owes you. Over a very large number of flips the ratio approaches 50/50, but that's a property of the long run, not a force correcting the next flip.

Key takeaway

Probability describes long-run frequency, not a "balancing force." Independent events don't influence each other; conditioning on an event shrinks your universe to just the cases where that event is true.

Chapter 4

Combinatorics & the Binomial Distribution

You run an experiment with a 10% chance of success on each try. Try it 5 times — what's the chance of at least one success? Your gut says "50%." Counting correctly says something quite different, and that gap is exactly what this chapter fixes.

What you think

If each independent try has a 10% chance of success, then 5 tries gives a 5 × 10% = 50% chance of at least one success.

What's actually true

Probabilities of independent events don't add up like that — you'd double count the chance of getting two or more successes. The correct approach uses the complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(zero successes) = 1 − (0.9)⁵ ≈ 41%, noticeably less than the naive 50%.

🎟️

The lottery-ticket analogy

Buying 5 lottery tickets doesn't give you 5× the winning chance in a simple additive sense — it raises your odds of winning at least once, but some of that probability "overlaps" with the chance of winning on more than one ticket. The honest way to count is through the complement: 1 minus the chance you lose every single time.

👥

The team-roster analogy

"How many different 3-person teams can I pick from 8 people?" doesn't care about the order you pick them in — Alex-then-Sam-then-Lee is the same team as Lee-then-Alex-then-Sam. That's a combination (written ⁸C₃), the building block behind the binomial formula, which counts how many different ways k successes can land among n tries.

Reveal the why — why does the shape shift when you move p?

When p is small, low success-counts are far more likely than high ones, so the bars pile up on the left (right-skewed). As p approaches 0.5, the distribution becomes symmetric — equally easy to land above or below the middle. Push p above 0.5 and the skew flips to the left. The "expected" number of successes is always n × p, which is why the peak of the bars slides along with that slider.

📐 The Math — factorials, combinations, and the binomial formula

A factorial counts how many ways to arrange n distinct items in order:

n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … × 2 × 1   (0! = 1, by convention)

A combination — "n choose k" — counts how many ways to pick k items from n when order doesn't matter:

ⁿCₖ = n! / [ k! × (n−k)! ]

The full binomial probability formula combines that count with how likely any one specific arrangement is:

P(k successes) = ⁿCₖ × pᵏ × (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

The mean and variance of a binomial distribution have clean closed forms — no simulation needed:

E[X] = np     Var(X) = np(1−p)
Worked example

A feature flag is shown to 4 users, each with a 25% (p=0.25) chance of clicking it. What's P(exactly 2 clicks)?

  1. ⁴C₂ = 4! / (2!×2!) = 24 / (2×2) = 6 ways to choose which 2 of the 4 click
  2. p² = 0.25² = 0.0625; (1−p)² = 0.75² = 0.5625
  3. P(2) = 6 × 0.0625 × 0.5625 = 6 × 0.03516
P(exactly 2 clicks) ≈ 0.211, or about 21.1%
⚡ Common sticking point

People confuse "n choose k" (how many ways k successes can be arranged among n tries) with the full binomial probability (how likely that count actually is). The combination count is just one ingredient — you still have to multiply by p^k × (1−p)^(n−k) to account for how likely any one specific arrangement of successes and failures actually is. Counting arrangements and weighing their likelihood are two separate steps that both matter.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

Multiplying probability by the number of tries double-counts the cases where more than one try succeeds. The clean way to compute "at least one success" is through the complement rule: figure out the probability that every single try fails, (1−p)ⁿ, then subtract that from 1. This correctly accounts for all the ways success could happen without any double-counting.

The binomial distribution describes the count of successes across n independent yes/no trials. Use the complement rule for "at least one" questions, and remember the expected count is always n × p — the peak the bars naturally settle around.

Chapter 5

The Normal Curve & Z-scores

Heights, measurement errors, IQ scores, response-time noise — wildly different things all settle into the same bell shape. That's not a coincidence; it's what happens whenever a value is the sum of many small, independent nudges.

What you think

"Normal distribution" means the data is somehow correct or expected — values outside the bell are abnormal or wrong.

What's actually true

"Normal" is a historical name for a specific symmetric bell shape, not a moral judgment. Plenty of perfectly valid real-world data (income, file sizes, time-to-failure) is not normal at all — it's skewed, and that's also completely normal.

🎲

The Galton board analogy

Drop a ball through a triangle of pegs — at each peg it bounces left or right by pure chance. Drop thousands of balls and the pile at the bottom forms a bell curve, automatically. The Central Limit idea, in physical form: many small independent nudges sum to a bell.

📐

The z-score ruler analogy

A z-score re-measures a value in units of "how many standard deviations from the mean," like converting inches and centimeters onto the same ruler. A z-score of +2 means "two typical-spreads above average" — comparable across totally different scales (test scores vs. salaries vs. load times).

Reveal the why — why is ±1σ always ~68%?

The shape of the normal curve is fixed by a single formula (it only depends on the mean and standard deviation), so the proportion of area between any two z-scores is always the same, no matter what the underlying units are — test scores, milliseconds, centimeters. ±1σ ≈ 68%, ±2σ ≈ 95%, ±3σ ≈ 99.7% are just fixed facts about that one curve shape, memorized as the "68-95-99.7 rule."

📐 The Math — the normal PDF and standardization

The normal curve's exact height at any point x is given by its probability density function — note it only depends on two numbers, the mean μ and standard deviation σ:

f(x) = [ 1 / (σ√(2π)) ] × e^[ −(x−μ)² / (2σ²) ]

You almost never compute this by hand — instead you standardize a value into a z-score, then look up (or compute) the area under a single universal curve:

z = (x − μ) / σ

To go back the other way — find the raw value at a given z-score — rearrange the formula:

x = μ + zσ
Worked example

API response times are normally distributed with μ = 200ms and σ = 30ms. What z-score does a 260ms response have, and roughly what percentile is that?

  1. z = (260 − 200) / 30 = 60/30 = 2.0
  2. From the 68-95-99.7 rule, z = 2 sits at the upper edge of the ±2σ band (≈95% of data within it)
  3. That leaves about 5% outside ±2σ, split evenly across both tails → ~2.5% above z=2
A 260ms response is around the 97.5th percentile — unusually slow
⚡ Common sticking point

People treat the 68-95-99.7 rule as universal — but it only applies if the data is actually approximately normal. Apply it to a heavily skewed distribution (like household income or website session length) and the percentages are simply wrong. Always sanity-check the shape of your data (a histogram takes ten seconds) before reaching for z-score rules.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

A z-score of −2 means the value sits two standard deviations below the mean — it's notably lower than typical, in the bottom ~2.5% of the distribution if the data is roughly normal. It converts a raw number into "how unusual is this," using the data's own natural unit of spread instead of an arbitrary scale.

The normal curve isn't a value judgment — it's one specific symmetric shape that emerges from summing many small independent effects. Chapter 6

Sampling & the Central Limit Theorem

You can't survey every user, log every request, or measure every product off the line. You measure a sample and hope it tells you about the whole. The Central Limit Theorem is the reason that hope is justified — even when the underlying data is a complete mess.

What you think

For sample averages to behave predictably (normally), the original population data has to already be normally distributed.

What's actually true

The Central Limit Theorem works even when the population is wildly skewed (like response times, which pile up near zero with a long tail). Take many samples, average each one, and those averages form a bell curve — regardless of the original shape, as long as the sample size is reasonably large.

🍪

The cookie-jar analogy

One cookie from the jar might be huge or tiny — wildly variable. But grab a handful of 30 cookies and weigh the average weight of the handful — do that over and over, and those handful-averages cluster tightly around the jar's true average weight, far more predictably than any single cookie.

🌊

The ripple-averaging analogy

A single wave height is chaotic. Average wave height over a 10-minute window is far steadier — random ups and downs partially cancel out. Larger samples average out more noise, which is why bigger samples give tighter, more trustworthy estimates.

Step-by-step walkthrough · Watch the CLT happen

We start with a population of response times that's heavily right-skewed — most requests are fast, a few are very slow.

Reveal the why — why does averaging "fix" the skew?

A single slow outlier can dominate one sample's average, but across many different samples, sometimes the outlier lands in the sample and sometimes it doesn't — and the extreme highs get diluted by the many ordinary values sitting alongside them in each sample. Average enough samples, and the sample-to-sample variation in "got an outlier or didn't" smooths into a symmetric bell, even though no single original measurement was ever symmetric.

📐 The Math — the formal CLT statement and standard error

Formally, the Central Limit Theorem says: for a population with any shape, mean μ, and standard deviation σ, the distribution of sample means (sample size n) approaches a normal distribution as n grows:

X̄ ~ approximately Normal( μ, σ/√n )   for large n

That σ/√n term is the standard error — the standard deviation of the sample mean itself (not of individual data points):

SE = σ / √n

This single formula explains why "collect more data" reduces noise: SE shrinks as n grows, but only at the rate of √n — quadrupling your sample only halves your error.

Worked example

Response times have population std. dev. σ = 40ms. Compare the standard error of the sample mean at two sample sizes.

  1. n = 25: SE = 40/√25 = 40/5 = 8ms
  2. n = 400: SE = 40/√400 = 40/20 = 2ms
A 16× larger sample (25 → 400) only shrinks the error by 4× (8ms → 2ms) — that's the √n relationship in action
⚡ Common sticking point

People confuse "the CLT makes individual data points normal" with the actual claim: it's the distribution of sample means (or sums) that becomes approximately normal, not the raw data itself. Your raw response-time logs stay skewed forever — it's only when you repeatedly compute averages of samples that the bell shape appears.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

The Central Limit Theorem guarantees that, regardless of the shape of the original data, the distribution of sample averages (across many repeated samples) becomes approximately normal as the sample size grows. That predictable bell shape is what lets us build confidence intervals and run hypothesis tests on the average, even when individual measurements are noisy and skewed.

Chapter 7

Hypothesis Testing & p-values

Your new ranking model scores higher on the test set. Is that a real improvement, or did it just get lucky on this particular slice of data? Hypothesis testing is the disciplined way to ask "how surprising would this result be if nothing had actually changed?"

What you think

A p-value of 0.03 means there's only a 3% chance the new model is genuinely no better than the old one — a 97% chance my model really did improve.

What's actually true

A p-value of 0.03 means: if the new model were truly no better (the null hypothesis is true), there'd be a 3% chance of seeing a difference this large or larger purely by random luck. It says nothing directly about the probability that the null hypothesis is true — that's a completely different, much harder question.

⚖️

The courtroom analogy

The null hypothesis is "innocent until proven guilty" — assume no effect exists. You don't try to prove innocence; you try to gather evidence so strong that "innocent" becomes hard to believe. A low p-value is like overwhelming evidence — it makes the "nothing changed" story implausible, not literally impossible.

🚨

The smoke-alarm analogy

A significance threshold (α = 0.05) is like setting a smoke alarm's sensitivity. Set it too sensitive and it false-alarms on burnt toast (false positive — claiming an effect that isn't real). Set it too relaxed and it misses an actual fire (false negative — missing a real effect). There's no "perfectly safe" setting, only a trade-off.

The p-value is the shaded area — the chance of a result this extreme, assuming the null hypothesis is exactly true.

📐 The Math — the test statistic, step by step

For comparing a sample mean against a known or hypothesized value (with known population σ), the z-test statistic measures how many standard errors apart your observation is from the null hypothesis's claim:

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)   = (x̄ − μ₀) / SE

For comparing two independent sample proportions (e.g. A/B test conversion rates), the formula extends naturally using a pooled standard error:

z = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) / √[ p̂(1−p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]

Once you have z, the p-value comes from the area in the tail(s) of the standard normal curve beyond that z — exactly the shaded region from Chapter 5's bell diagram.

Worked example — A/B test on conversion rate

Control: 50/1000 converted (5.0%). Variant: 70/1000 converted (7.0%). Pooled rate p̂ = 120/2000 = 0.06.

  1. SE = √[ 0.06×0.94×(1/1000 + 1/1000) ] = √[0.0564 × 0.002] = √0.0001128 ≈ 0.01062
  2. z = (0.07 − 0.05) / 0.01062 = 0.02 / 0.01062 ≈ 1.88
  3. Two-tailed p-value for z ≈ 1.88 is about 0.06
p ≈ 0.06 — above the common α = 0.05 threshold, so this particular result falls just short of "statistically significant" at that level
⚡ Common sticking point

"Not statistically significant" gets misread as "proven there's no effect." It actually just means the evidence wasn't strong enough to confidently rule out "no effect" — which can happen because there's truly no effect, or because your sample was too small to detect a real but modest one. Absence of significance is not evidence of absence; it's often evidence you need more data.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

Chapter 8

Effect Size & Statistical Power

A p-value tells you whether you should believe an effect exists. It tells you nothing about whether that effect is big enough to care about, or whether your test was even capable of detecting it if it did exist. Two separate, equally important questions.

What you think

"p < 0.001" means the effect must be large and important — the smaller the p-value, the bigger the real-world impact.

What's actually true

With a large enough sample, even a trivially tiny, practically meaningless difference can produce an extremely small p-value. P-value reflects confidence that an effect isn't exactly zero; effect size (like Cohen's d, or simply "the difference in conversion rate") is the separate number that tells you whether that effect actually matters.

🔬

The magnifying-glass analogy

Statistical significance with a huge sample is like an extremely powerful microscope — zoom in far enough and you'll spot a speck of dust on a otherwise spotless lens. Finding the speck (a "significant" result) doesn't mean the speck is ruining your photos. Effect size asks "how big is the speck," not just "can we detect it."

🎧

The hearing-test analogy

Statistical power is like the sensitivity of a hearing test. A high-power test (often: a bigger sample) can pick up a faint true signal; a low-power test might miss it entirely and wrongly conclude "no effect," even though a real one was there all along — like a hearing test too crude to detect a soft tone that's genuinely audible to a better instrument.

Reveal the why — why does a bigger effect mean more power?

Power is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when it truly exists — visually, it's the portion of the "true effect" curve that lands past your significance threshold. As the true effect grows, its whole curve shifts further from the null, so more of it clears the threshold automatically. The same logic applies to sample size: a larger sample shrinks the spread of both curves, separating them more cleanly and increasing the overlap that clears the line.

📐 The Math — Cohen's d and what determines power

Cohen's d standardizes a difference between two means into units of "how many standard deviations apart," making it comparable across totally different metrics:

d = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / σ_pooled

Conventionally: d ≈ 0.2 is a small effect, d ≈ 0.5 medium, d ≈ 0.8 large — though what counts as "practically important" always depends on context.

Power isn't free-floating — it's determined by four interlocking knobs, and you can solve for any one if you fix the other three:

Power ↑ as: effect size (d) ↑, sample size (n) ↑, significance level (α) ↑, or noise (σ) ↓
Worked example

Two model variants score 82% and 85% accuracy on a benchmark, with a pooled standard deviation of 6 percentage points across repeated runs.

  1. d = (85 − 82) / 6 = 3/6 = 0.5
d = 0.5 is a "medium" effect — worth investigating further, but you'd want a reasonably large sample of runs to reliably detect it, since medium effects need more power than large ones to clear a significance threshold
⚡ Common sticking point

"We didn't find a significant result, so there's no effect" is one of the most common statistical mistakes — and it's often actually a power problem. An underpowered study (too few samples, or too small an expected effect) can easily fail to detect a real, meaningful effect. Before trusting a null result, ask: did this test have enough power to find the effect if it existed?

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

Statistical significance only tells you the observed effect is unlikely to be pure noise — it says nothing about the size of that effect. With a very large sample, even a tiny, real but practically irrelevant difference can produce a very small p-value. Effect size is the separate measurement that tells you whether the difference is actually large enough to matter in practice.

Significance and importance are different questions. Always report effect size alongside a p-value, and before trusting a "no effect" result, check whether the test had enough power to detect a real effect in the first place.

Chapter 9

Confidence Intervals & Correlation

The expert move in statistics isn't getting a single number — it's reporting a range that's honest about your uncertainty, and knowing the difference between "these two things move together" and "one of them causes the other."

What you think

A "95% confidence interval" means there's a 95% probability the true value lies inside this specific interval I calculated.

What's actually true

The 95% refers to the procedure: if you repeated the sampling process many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. Any one interval either contains the true value or it doesn't — the 95% describes the method's long-run reliability, not this single interval's odds.

🎣

The fishing-net analogy

A confidence interval is a net, not a pin. A 95%-reliable net design will successfully catch the true fish in about 95 out of 100 casts — but you only get to make one cast in real life, and you never get to see if that particular cast caught the fish or missed.

☂️

The umbrella-sales analogy

Umbrella sales and rain both rise together — they're correlated. But umbrellas don't cause rain, and rain doesn't directly cause umbrella manufacturing. A hidden third factor (rainy season) drives both. Correlation tells you two things move together; it never tells you which, if either, is the cause.

Reveal the why — why doesn't correlation imply causation?

Two variables can move together for at least three reasons: A causes B, B causes A, or a hidden third variable C causes both A and B (a "confounder"). A correlation coefficient r can't distinguish between these — it only measures how tightly two variables move together in a straight-line sense. Distinguishing cause from coincidence requires either controlled experiments (randomized A/B tests) or careful causal reasoning about confounders, not just a bigger r value.

📐 The Math — confidence intervals and Pearson's r

A confidence interval is just a point estimate plus-or-minus a margin of error built from the standard error and a critical value:

CI = x̄ ± (critical value) × SE   e.g. x̄ ± 1.96 × (σ/√n) for 95% with known σ

When σ is unknown (the usual real-world case) and the sample is small, you use the t-distribution instead of the normal — it has slightly fatter tails to account for the extra uncertainty of also estimating σ from the sample:

CI = x̄ ± t* × (s/√n)   t* depends on confidence level and degrees of freedom (n−1)

Pearson's correlation coefficient r measures the strength of a straight-line relationship between two variables:

r = Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)] / √[ Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² × Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)² ]

Squaring r gives R² — the proportion of variance in one variable "explained" by its linear relationship with the other.

Worked example

A sample of n = 16 page-load measurements has mean x̄ = 220ms and sample std. dev. s = 24ms. For a 95% CI, t* ≈ 2.13 (for 15 degrees of freedom).

  1. SE = s/√n = 24/√16 = 24/4 = 6ms
  2. Margin = t* × SE = 2.13 × 6 ≈ 12.8ms
95% CI ≈ [207.2ms, 232.8ms] — the method that builds intervals this way captures the true mean about 95% of the time it's used
⚡ Common sticking point

Engineers often report "the average load time improved" without a confidence interval and treat it as settled fact, forgetting that the average itself is a noisy estimate from one sample of traffic. Always ask: how wide is the interval around this number? A tiny improvement with a wide, overlapping interval might just be noise; the interval is what tells you whether the result is solid or shaky.

Now explain it in your own words

Model answer

A correlation of 0.9 only tells you A and B move together tightly in a straight-line pattern. It doesn't reveal the mechanism — A could cause B, B could cause A, or a hidden third factor could be driving both. Establishing causation usually requires a controlled experiment or strong reasoning that rules out confounders and reverse causation, not just a high correlation number.
