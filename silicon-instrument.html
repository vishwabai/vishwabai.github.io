0x01Foundations

Everything is a switch that a clock keeps poking

Before CPU, GPU, or TPU mean anything, you need one idea: a computer is an enormous pile of tiny switches, arranged so that flipping some of them flips others in useful patterns — and a metronome called the clock that tells the whole pile "now… now… now" billions of times a second.

The transistor: the atom of computing

A transistor is a switch with no moving parts. It has an input, an output, and a gate. Put a voltage on the gate and current flows (a 1); remove it and current stops (a 0). That's it. No magic — just a valve for electricity that another wire can open and close. A modern chip has tens of billions of these, each far smaller than a virus.

≈
Analogy — dominoes vs water valves Think of each transistor as a water valve where the handle is itself turned by water pressure from another pipe. Chain them cleverly and the flow of water in one place decides the flow everywhere else. Computation is just water (electrons) being routed by valves (transistors) that route each other.

From switches to logic

Wire two switches in series and current only passes when both are on — you've built an AND gate. Wire them in parallel and current passes when either is on — an OR gate. Add a clever inverter and you get XOR, the gate that says "one or the other, but not both." From AND, OR, and NOT you can build anything a computer does — arithmetic, memory, decisions. Poke the gates below and watch logic emerge from switches.

LAB · Build logic from switches

Flip the two input switches. Watch how the same two bits produce different answers depending on how the transistors are wired.
Both inputs are 0 — every gate is quiet.
✕
Misconception "Computers understand numbers and letters." They don't. Deep down there are only high and low voltages. The number 42, the letter 'A', this sentence, and a cat photo are all just agreed-upon patterns of on/off switches. Meaning lives in the convention, not the silicon.

The clock: the heartbeat

Switches settle at different speeds, so a chip can't just let signals ripple freely — it would be chaos. Instead a clock sends a square wave through the whole chip: tick (rise), tock (fall), over and over. On each tick, every part of the chip latches its result and moves to the next step in lockstep. "3.5 GHz" means 3.5 billion ticks per second — 3.5 billion chances every second for the machine to take one more baby step of computation.

LAB · The clock that drives everything

Drag the frequency. The waveform is the drumbeat; the counter is what gets done each beat. Faster clock = more steps per second — but as you'll learn, more steps isn't the whole story.
At 2.0 GHz, one tick lasts 0.50 nanoseconds. In that time, light travels only 15 cm.
★
The one idea to keep A processor is a machine that, on every clock tick, reads an instruction, does the tiny thing it says, and moves on. CPU, GPU, and TPU differ entirely in how many of these tiny things they do per tick, and what kind. Everything else in this course is detail on that theme.

Explain it back

In your own words: if there are only voltages inside a chip, where does the number "5" actually live?

The number 5 doesn't "live" anywhere as a 5. It exists as a pattern of on/off voltages — e.g. 00000101 — on a set of wires, plus a shared agreement that this pattern means five. Change the agreement (say, treat it as a color or a pixel) and the identical voltages mean something else. The silicon holds switches; meaning is a convention layered on top.

The CPU

The latency machine. A CPU's whole design philosophy is to finish one chain of dependent steps as fast as physically possible. It is a genius that does things one-at-a-time, brilliantly.

0x02CPU · The engine loop

Fetch, decode, execute — forever

A CPU runs one loop for its entire life. It reads the next instruction from memory (fetch), works out what it means (decode), does it (execute), and saves the result (writeback). Then it does it again. Billions of times a second. That loop is the beating heart of every program you've ever run.

To follow the loop you need to meet four parts of the core:

  • Program Counter (PC) — a pointer holding the address of the next instruction. Think of it as your finger on the current line of a recipe.
  • Registers — a handful of ultra-fast scratchpad slots (R1, R2, …) right next to the math unit. A CPU has only dozens of them, but they're the fastest storage in the universe of the chip.
  • ALU (Arithmetic Logic Unit) — the calculator. Adds, subtracts, compares, ANDs, ORs.
  • Control Unit — the conductor that decodes each instruction and tells everyone else what to do this tick.

◆
Going deeper — what an "instruction" really is An instruction like ADD R3, R1, R2 is, in the chip, just a number — say 0x00232820. Certain bits are the opcode ("this is an ADD"), others name the source and destination registers. The decoder is a big lookup table that turns those bit-fields into control signals. Assembly language is just human-readable names for these numbers.
✕
Misconception "The CPU runs my Python/JavaScript." It never sees them. Your high-level code is translated — by a compiler or interpreter — down into these tiny numeric instructions. The CPU only ever eats machine code. Everything above that is a translation layer for humans.
0x03CPU · The assembly-line trick

Pipelining: the single best idea in CPU design

If each instruction needs 5 phases, why let all 5 units sit idle waiting for one instruction to finish? A car factory doesn't build one car start-to-finish before starting the next. It runs an assembly line: while car 1 is getting doors, car 2 is getting an engine, car 3 a chassis. CPUs do exactly this with instructions.

Split the core into stages — Fetch → Decode → Execute → Memory → Writeback — and keep every stage busy on a different instruction. One instruction still takes 5 ticks to finish, but a new one completes every single tick. That's a near-5× throughput win from rearranging work you were already doing.

LAB · Watch the assembly line fill up

Toggle pipelining on and off. Each row is an instruction, each column a clock cycle, each colored block a pipeline stage. Notice how pipelining overlaps instructions instead of stacking them end-to-end.
Throughput: ~1 instruction / cycle
IF fetch ID decode EX execute MEM memory WB writeback
★
Two numbers you must not confuse Latency = how long one instruction takes start to finish (still 5 ticks). Throughput = how many finish per tick (now ~1). Pipelining doesn't make a single instruction faster — it makes the stream faster. This latency-vs-throughput split is the exact fault line between CPUs and GPUs, so hold onto it.
◆
Going deeper — why chips have "deep" pipelines Modern CPUs split work into 14–20 stages, not 5. Shorter stages mean each does less per tick, so the clock can tick faster. But there's a catch you'll meet next: the deeper the pipeline, the more work is thrown away when the CPU guesses wrong about what comes next. Pipeline depth is a tug-of-war between clock speed and the cost of being wrong.
0x04CPU · When the line jams

Hazards, guessing, and running ahead of yourself

The assembly line only flows if every instruction is independent and known in advance. Real code isn't. Sometimes instruction 4 needs the result of instruction 3 (which isn't done yet). Sometimes the code says "if X, go here, else go there" and the CPU doesn't yet know X. These are hazards, and how CPUs fight them is where the deep engineering lives.

Data hazards & the stall

If ADD R3,R1,R2 is immediately followed by MUL R4,R3,R5, the MUL needs R3 — but the ADD hasn't written it back yet. The pipeline must stall (insert idle "bubbles") until R3 is ready. CPUs soften this with forwarding: wiring the ALU's fresh result straight back to the next instruction before it's even saved, dodging most stalls.

Control hazards & branch prediction

When code branches (if, loops, function calls), the CPU doesn't know which instruction comes next until the branch resolves — several stages later. Rather than wait, it guesses and charges ahead speculatively. A hardware branch predictor — often a little learning machine tracking each branch's history — is right well over 95% of the time. When it's wrong, the CPU must flush the wrongly-fetched instructions and restart. On a deep pipeline that misprediction can cost 15–20 wasted ticks.

LAB · Feel the cost of a bad guess

This branch predictor learns a pattern. Feed it a predictable loop and it nails almost every prediction. Feed it random branches and watch it thrash — every miss flushes the pipeline and burns cycles.
Accuracy: —
A green tick = correct guess (line flows). A red cross = misprediction (pipeline flush, ~15 cycles wasted).

Out-of-order & superscalar: doing several at once

Two more tricks stack on top. A superscalar CPU has multiple copies of each unit, so it can fetch and execute several instructions per tick (4–6 is common). An out-of-order engine looks ahead in the instruction stream, finds work whose inputs are ready, and runs it now instead of waiting in program order — then quietly reassembles the results in the right order so your program never notices. This is why a single CPU core is so ferociously good at one messy, branchy task.

✕
Misconception "A 4 GHz CPU does 4 billion instructions per second." It can do more — a superscalar core retires several per tick — or far fewer, if it keeps stalling on memory or mispredicting branches. Clock speed is the drumbeat, not the amount of music. The real metric is IPC (instructions per cycle) × clock.

Explain it back

Why does a deeper pipeline (more stages) make branch mispredictions more expensive?

Because the CPU keeps fetching and starting new instructions speculatively while it waits to learn whether its guess was right. The more stages between the guess and its confirmation, the more speculative instructions are already in flight — and all of them must be thrown away and refetched when the guess was wrong. A 5-stage pipeline wastes a few cycles on a miss; a 20-stage pipeline can waste 15–20. Depth buys clock speed but raises the penalty for being wrong.
0x05CPU · The real bottleneck

The memory wall, and the caches built to hide it

Here's the dirty secret of modern computing: the CPU is almost never the slow part. Waiting for data is. Your processor can do arithmetic in a fraction of a nanosecond, but fetching a number from main memory takes hundreds of times longer. A CPU that had to hit RAM for every value would spend 99% of its life tapping its foot.

The fix is a stack of small, fast memories called caches sitting between the core and RAM. L1 is tiny and blindingly fast, right beside the ALU. L2 is bigger and a bit slower. L3 is shared across cores and slower still. When the core needs data, it checks L1, then L2, then L3, then finally RAM — hoping to find it early. A hit is cheap; a miss that reaches all the way to RAM is a catastrophe measured in hundreds of wasted cycles.

LAB · The latency ladder, at human scale

Real access times are too far apart to draw side by side, so here's the trick that makes them felt: imagine an L1 cache hit took one second. Everything else scales up from there. Suddenly a trip to main memory is a coffee break, and reaching an old spinning disk is a seasons-long expedition. (Bars are log-scaled; the human-scale time is the point.)
The gap between a cache hit and a RAM miss is why "cache-friendly" code can run many times faster than "correct but naive" code doing identical math.

Locality: why caches work at all

Caches would be useless if programs accessed memory randomly. They work because real programs have locality:

  • Temporal locality — if you used a value, you'll probably use it again soon (a loop counter, a running total). So the cache keeps recently-used data around.
  • Spatial locality — if you used address N, you'll probably use N+1 soon (walking through an array). So the cache never fetches one byte — it grabs a whole cache line (usually 64 bytes) at once, betting you'll want the neighbors.

LAB · Same math, 8× the speed — just from access order

This 2D array lives in memory row by row. Sum it row-major (across each row) and you march along contiguous memory — every cache line you pull is fully used. Sum it column-major (down each column) and you leap across memory, wasting most of every line you fetch. Identical additions. Wildly different cache behavior.
Cache misses: —
Green flashes = the value was already in cache (hit). Red flashes = a miss that stalled the core to fetch a fresh 64-byte line from RAM.
★
The most useful performance rule you'll ever learn Modern CPU performance is mostly about keeping the caches fed. Contiguous data, predictable access patterns, and structures that pack tightly into cache lines routinely beat "smarter" algorithms that jump around memory. When people say a program is "cache-friendly," this is what they mean — and it's often a bigger win than a better big-O.
◆
Going deeper — false sharing, the multicore trap Because caches move data in 64-byte lines, two CPU cores writing to two different variables that happen to sit in the same cache line will fight over it — each write forces the other core's copy to reload. Your code looks perfectly parallel and independent, yet it crawls. This is false sharing, and the fix is simply padding hot variables so they land on separate lines.
0x06CPU · More cores, more lanes

Three flavors of "do more at once"

A single core is already parallel inside (pipelining, superscalar). But there are three bigger levers for doing more simultaneously — and understanding them is the runway to the GPU.

Multicore

Put 8, 16, 64 complete cores on one chip. Each runs its own instruction stream. True parallelism, but only if your work splits into independent chunks — and coordinating them (locks, shared data) has real cost.

SMT / Hyperthreading

One physical core pretends to be two. When thread A stalls waiting on memory, the core instantly runs thread B's work in the idle slots. It doesn't double speed — it just stops wasting the bubbles.

SIMD (vectors)

Single Instruction, Multiple Data. One instruction adds 8 or 16 numbers at once through wide vector lanes (AVX, NEON). Perfect for the same operation over a big array — and the seed of the GPU's whole idea.

Notice the trend across these three: we keep finding ways to apply the same work to more data at once. SIMD does it with 8–16 lanes bolted onto a CPU core. A GPU asks: what if we did this with tens of thousands of lanes and made it the whole point of the machine? That question is the next part of the course.

✕
Misconception "More cores always means faster." Only for work that actually divides into independent pieces. By Amdahl's Law, if even 10% of a job must run sequentially, then no matter how many cores you throw at it you can never get more than a 10× speedup — the stubborn 10% dominates. Some problems simply refuse to parallelize.
0x07CPU · Practice

How to actually use a CPU well

Everything above becomes practical advice. You rarely control the hardware, but you completely control how your code meets it. Here's what actually moves the needle, roughly in order of impact.

1 · Measure before you touch anything

Intuition about performance is almost always wrong. Profile first, find the one hot loop that dominates, and ignore the rest. Optimizing code that runs 0.1% of the time is wasted effort no matter how clever it is.

2 · Respect the cache above all

Lay data out contiguously and walk it in order. Prefer arrays of values over scattered pointer-chasing structures. Keep hot data small and packed. This single habit often beats every other optimization combined, because the memory wall is the real enemy.

3 · Keep branches predictable in hot loops

Sorted or patterned data lets the branch predictor win; random conditions make it thrash. Sometimes rewriting an if as branchless arithmetic in a tight loop is a real speedup.

4 · Let the compiler vectorize — or help it

Simple, tight loops over contiguous arrays let the compiler emit SIMD automatically. Convoluted loops with hidden dependencies block it. Clean code is fast code here.

5 · Parallelize last, and only what divides cleanly

Threads add coordination cost and bugs. Reach for them when you have genuinely independent work and you've already made the single-threaded path cache-efficient.

★
The CPU in one sentence A CPU spends billions of transistors — on caches, predictors, and out-of-order engines — to make one messy sequential task finish as fast as possible. It is the right tool whenever the work is branchy, unpredictable, latency-sensitive, or hard to parallelize. When the work is instead thousands of identical independent operations, the CPU is the wrong tool — and that's the GPU's cue to enter.
Checkpoint · A program spends 90% of its time in a loop that sums a huge 2D array column-by-column and runs slowly. What's the highest-impact fix?
Column-by-column traversal of a row-major array leaps across memory, so nearly every access misses cache and stalls on RAM — the memory wall, not the math, is the bottleneck. Reordering to row-major makes each 64-byte cache line fully used, often an 8× win from a one-line change. Faster clocks, more cores, or hand assembly don't help when the core is just waiting on memory.

The GPU

The throughput machine. Where a CPU is a few brilliant geniuses, a GPU is ten thousand diligent clerks. No single one is fast — but ask them all the same question at once and they answer faster than any genius could.

0x08GPU · The opposite bet

Why a different machine had to exist

GPUs were born to draw pixels. A 4K screen has ~8 million pixels, and every frame you must compute a color for each — the same lighting math, 8 million times, with no pixel depending on its neighbor. A CPU doing them one-at-a-time, however cleverly, is hopeless. The task begged for a machine built the opposite way.

So the GPU makes a radical trade. It throws away most of the CPU's cleverness — the giant branch predictors, the deep out-of-order engines, the huge caches that make one thread fast — and spends that silicon on thousands of simple arithmetic cores instead. Each core is slow and dumb compared to a CPU core. But there are thousands, and they all work at once.

CPU philosophy

Minimize latency: finish one dependent chain as fast as possible. Spend transistors on caches and prediction to never let a single thread wait. A handful of very smart cores.

GPU philosophy

Maximize throughput: finish the most total work per second. Don't fight latency — hide it by always having thousands of other threads ready to run. A flood of simple cores.

Latency hiding: the trick that replaces caches

When a CPU thread stalls waiting on memory, the core sits mostly idle (SMT fills a little of it). A GPU's answer is brutally simple: when one group of threads stalls on memory, instantly switch to another group that's ready, and another, and another. With tens of thousands of threads resident, there's always someone ready to run. The memory latency never disappears — it's just hidden behind a mountain of other work. This is why GPUs need to be kept drowning in threads to perform.

★
The core trade in one line A CPU reduces the time each task waits. A GPU accepts the waiting and covers it with sheer volume. That's why GPUs are spectacular at big, uniform, independent workloads (graphics, matrix math, neural nets) and poor at branchy, sequential, one-thing-at-a-time logic — the exact inverse of a CPU.
✕
Misconception "A GPU is just a faster CPU." It is dramatically slower on any single sequential task — one GPU core might run at a third of a CPU core's speed with none of its cleverness. The GPU only wins when the same operation applies to enormous amounts of independent data. On your everyday branchy code it would lose badly.
0x09GPU · The execution model

SIMT: thousands of threads marching in lockstep

A GPU doesn't schedule its thousands of threads individually — that'd need thousands of expensive control units. Instead it herds them into groups (NVIDIA calls a group of 32 a warp) and runs the whole group off a single instruction. All 32 threads execute the identical instruction at the identical tick, each on its own slice of data. This is SIMT — Single Instruction, Multiple Threads.

It's an elegant cheat: one control unit steers 32 arithmetic lanes. That's how you afford thousands of cores — you share the "brain" across whole warps and spend the saved silicon on more raw math.

LAB · A warp executes — and then diverges

Each square is a thread. In lockstep mode they all run the same instruction together — beautifully efficient. But hit a branch where threads disagree (if(data > 0) where only some are), and the warp must run the if path with half the threads masked off, then the else path with the other half. The lanes that aren't active just idle. That's warp divergence, and it can halve your throughput.
Efficiency: —
Bright = actively computing this cycle. Dim = masked off / idle. Lockstep keeps every lane bright; divergence forces lanes to take turns.

The hierarchy: threads, blocks, and SMs

Threads are grouped into blocks; blocks are handed to physical units called Streaming Multiprocessors (SMs) — each SM being a cluster of cores with its own scheduler and a patch of ultra-fast on-chip shared memory. A big GPU has dozens of SMs, each juggling many warps, switching between them every time one stalls. When you write GPU code, you're really describing what one thread does, then launching millions of copies of it across this hierarchy.

★
Why "avoid branches" is GPU gospel On a CPU, a branch is cheap (predict it and move on). On a GPU, a branch where threads in the same warp disagree forces the warp to execute both sides one after the other, wasting the idle lanes. Uniform, branch-free work over uniform data is what keeps all 32 lanes lit. This is the single biggest mindset shift moving from CPU to GPU programming.
◆
Going deeper — tensor cores Modern GPUs bolt on special units called tensor cores that do nothing but small matrix multiply-accumulates in one shot, often in reduced precision. Because deep learning is 90% matrix multiply, these units deliver most of a modern GPU's AI performance — and they're the GPU quietly borrowing the TPU's whole idea, which you're about to meet.
0x0AGPU · Feeding the beast

Bandwidth is king — and how you access memory decides everything

A GPU's thousands of cores have a bottomless appetite for data. Keeping them fed needs enormous memory bandwidth — which is why GPUs use exotic stacked memory (HBM) delivering terabytes per second. But raw bandwidth only helps if your threads request memory the way the hardware wants.

The memory hierarchy, GPU-style

  • Registers — per-thread, instant. Thousands per SM, split among resident threads.
  • Shared memory — per-block, on-chip, very fast, and programmer-controlled. This is your secret weapon: manually stage data here so a whole block reuses it instead of hammering global memory.
  • L2 cache — shared across the chip.
  • Global memory (HBM) — huge (tens of GB) but hundreds of cycles away. Every trip here is expensive; the game is to make as few as possible.

LAB · Coalesced vs scattered access

When the 32 threads of a warp read consecutive addresses, the hardware fuses them into one fat memory transaction — full bandwidth. When they read scattered addresses, it needs a separate transaction per thread — bandwidth collapses. Same amount of data, up to 32× the memory traffic.
Transactions: —
Each arrow is a thread reaching into memory. Coalesced accesses group under one bracket (one transaction); scattered ones each need their own.
◆
Going deeper — the PCIe tax A GPU is a separate device with its own memory. Your data starts in CPU RAM and must be copied across the PCIe bus to the GPU before it can be touched — and that link is far slower than the GPU's internal bandwidth. This is why a small job is often slower on a GPU: you spend more time shipping data back and forth than computing. The rule: move data to the GPU once and keep it there, doing as much work as possible before bringing results back.
0x0BGPU · Practice

How to actually use a GPU well

You may never write raw CUDA — libraries like PyTorch hide it — but the same handful of truths decide whether your GPU flies or crawls, whether you're training a model or writing a kernel by hand.

1 · Give it a mountain of parallel work

GPUs hide latency with sheer thread count. A half-empty GPU is a slow GPU. This is exactly why larger batch sizes often train faster per-item — you're keeping more lanes lit. Tiny workloads can't fill the machine and waste it.

2 · Move data once, compute a lot

The PCIe trip dominates small jobs. Keep tensors resident on the GPU, chain operations there, and avoid needless .cpu() / .numpy() round-trips that silently stall everything to shuttle data back.

3 · Keep warps uniform — dodge divergence

Data-dependent branches where neighboring threads disagree serialize the warp. Prefer uniform operations over uniform data; sort or bucket data so threads in a warp take the same path.

4 · Access memory in coalesced, contiguous patterns

Lay out data so consecutive threads touch consecutive addresses. This one habit can swing memory-bound kernels by an order of magnitude.

5 · Prefer big matrix ops — they hit the tensor cores

Framing work as large matrix multiplies lets the tensor cores do the heavy lifting. Many small ops leave that specialized hardware idle. Fuse where you can.

★
The GPU in one sentence A GPU wins when you have thousands-to-millions of identical, independent operations and enough of them to keep the machine drowning in work — graphics, simulations, and above all the dense matrix math of neural networks. Feed it small, branchy, or sequential work and it starves. Which raises a question: if AI is mostly matrix multiply, why not build a chip that does only that? Enter the TPU.

The TPU

The matrix machine. If the GPU is a general-purpose parallel workhorse, the TPU is a scalpel: a chip that does almost nothing except multiply matrices — and does it with an elegance that borders on the beautiful.

0x0CTPU · The specialist's bet

When you know exactly what you'll compute, build the chip for it

Google noticed something around 2013: if their neural networks kept growing, they'd need to double every datacenter just to serve them on CPUs. But those networks spent almost all their time doing one operation — matrix multiplication. So they asked the specialist's question: why pay for a flexible chip when the workload never changes?

A TPU (Tensor Processing Unit) is an ASIC — an Application-Specific Integrated Circuit. It throws away even more generality than a GPU did. No support for branchy code, no rendering hardware, a stripped-down instruction set. Nearly all its silicon is poured into one gigantic matrix-multiply unit. Give up doing everything, and you can do one thing with staggering efficiency per watt and per dollar.

CPU

Does anything. Flexible, latency-optimized, general-purpose. Jack of all trades.

GPU

Does parallel things. Thousands of cores for any data-parallel workload — graphics, science, AI.

TPU

Does matrix multiplies. Almost nothing else — but at an efficiency general chips can't touch.

★
The specialization spectrum This is the master pattern of all computing hardware: generality costs efficiency. Every step from CPU → GPU → TPU trades flexibility for speed and power on a narrower job. A TPU can't run your operating system or even most of your Python — but for the matrix math at the heart of deep learning, nothing general-purpose keeps up.
0x0DTPU · The heart of the machine

The systolic array: computation as a heartbeat

Here is the idea that makes a TPU a TPU — and once it clicks, you'll see why it's so efficient. The word systolic comes from systole, the contraction of a heart. Data doesn't get fetched, computed, and stored one value at a time. Instead it pulses rhythmically through a grid of tiny multiply-and-add cells, each cell handing its results to the next like a bucket brigade.

Why this is the whole trick

In a normal chip, the expensive part of arithmetic isn't the math — it's moving data to and from memory. Every fetch costs energy and time. The systolic array's genius is data reuse: a value read from memory once flows through an entire row or column of cells, feeding dozens of multiplications before it ever needs to be read again. The math happens as data streams past, without constant trips back to memory. That's how a TPU does enormous matrix multiplies while sipping power.

LAB · Watch a matrix multiply flow through the grid

This is a 3×3 systolic array computing C = A × B. Rows of A pulse in from the left; columns of B pulse down from the top. They meet inside each cell, which multiplies them and accumulates the running sum in place. Watch the diagonal wavefront sweep through — that's the heartbeat. When it finishes, every cell holds one finished number of the answer, and no input was ever re-read from memory.

Going deeper — real TPUs are 128×128 This demo shows a 3×3 grid for clarity. A real TPU's matrix unit is a 128×128 array — over 16,000 multiply-accumulate cells firing every cycle, doing a 128×128 matrix multiply as data washes through. Weights are loaded and held stationary in the cells while activations stream past, so each weight is reused across an entire stream of inputs. That's tens of thousands of multiplications per tick from a single wave of data.
✕
Misconception "A TPU is just a GPU for Google." Architecturally they're quite different. A GPU is thousands of independent programmable cores running threads; a TPU is one enormous hardwired matrix engine with data flowing through it in lockstep. The GPU is a flexible parallel computer; the TPU is closer to a purpose-built matrix-multiply pipeline cast into silicon.
0x0ETPU · Precision & practice

Cheaper numbers, and how to use a TPU well

There's a second reason TPUs are so efficient: they don't bother with full precision. A CPU computes in exact 32- or 64-bit numbers because a bank balance can't be approximate. But a neural network is a giant approximation already — it barely notices if its numbers are a little fuzzy. So TPUs compute in low precision, and that's a superpower.

Why smaller numbers win

A smaller number takes less silicon to multiply, less energy to move, and less memory bandwidth to ship. Halve the bits and you can roughly double the throughput and the model you can fit. Google's own format, bfloat16, is the clever trick here: it keeps the full range of 32-bit floats (so training doesn't blow up) but throws away precision bits it doesn't need — half the size, nearly the same training behavior. For inference, chips go further to int8 or lower.

◆
Going deeper — this is what "quantization" means When you hear a model has been quantized, this is it: its weights were converted from 16/32-bit floats down to 8-bit (or 4-bit) integers. The model gets smaller and faster with usually a tiny accuracy cost — which is why quantization is how big models get squeezed onto phones and edge chips. TPUs made low-precision math a first-class citizen; the whole industry followed.

Using a TPU well — the essentials

Make everything a big, regular matrix multiply. The systolic array is a fixed grid; the more fully you fill it, the more of the chip does useful work. Dimensions that are multiples of the array size (128) waste nothing.

Use large batches. Streaming many inputs past stationary weights is exactly what the array is built for. Small batches leave most of the heartbeat idle.

Embrace bfloat16. It's the native currency. Fighting to keep everything in 32-bit throws away the TPU's biggest advantage.

Let the compiler (XLA) fuse your graph. TPUs shine when a whole model is compiled into one efficient pipeline rather than dispatched op-by-op. Frameworks like JAX and TensorFlow are built for this.

Keep data off the host. Like the GPU's PCIe tax, shuttling data between the host CPU and the TPU stalls the array. Feed it in a steady stream.

★
The TPU in one sentence A TPU is a chip that gave up nearly all flexibility to make one operation — large matrix multiplication in low precision — as fast and power-efficient as physically possible. It's the ultimate expression of "know your workload, then build the silicon for it." When your workload is huge, regular, matrix-heavy deep learning at scale, nothing sips less power per multiply.
Checkpoint · What single property of the systolic array makes it so energy-efficient at matrix multiply?
The dominant cost in arithmetic hardware is moving data, not the multiply itself. A systolic array reads each input from memory once and lets it pulse through an entire row or column of cells, feeding many multiply-accumulates before anything returns to memory. This data reuse — not clock speed, transistor count, or precision — is the heart of its efficiency. (And TPUs actually favor low precision, not 64-bit.)

0x10Together

Real systems use all three at once

The most important truth in modern computing: these chips don't compete, they collaborate. Every serious system is heterogeneous — different silicon for different shapes of work, coordinated by the CPU. This is called heterogeneous computing, and it's the whole game now.

A day in the life of training a model

  1. The CPU reads training data from disk, decodes and shuffles it, and preprocesses batches — messy, branchy, sequential work it's perfect for.
  2. It ships each batch to the GPU or TPU, which runs the forward and backward passes — a torrent of matrix multiplies they were born for.
  3. The accelerator streams gradients back; the CPU coordinates checkpoints, logging, and the next batch — keeping the expensive accelerator fed so it never idles.

The engineering skill is keeping the fast chip busy. An idle GPU waiting on a slow CPU data pipeline is the most common (and expensive) failure in real ML systems — the accelerator is only worth it if the conductor keeps the music flowing.

★
Even your phone is heterogeneous The chip in your pocket is a system-on-chip: CPU cores for apps, a GPU for graphics, and an NPU (neural processing unit — a tiny TPU-like matrix engine) for on-device AI like face unlock and photo processing. Three philosophies, one piece of silicon, each handed the work it's best at. Learn to see this division of labor and you understand modern hardware.
0x11The road ahead

Why the hardware era is genuinely back

Your instinct that hardware matters again is right — and there's a precise reason, not just hype. For thirty years, software could ignore hardware because chips got faster for free every 18 months. That free lunch is over, and it changes everything.

Two laws broke, and the world pivoted

Dennard scaling — the rule that shrinking transistors also cut their power — quietly died around 2006. Transistors kept getting smaller, but the power per area stopped falling. Crank the clock higher and the chip melts. That's the power wall, and it's why clock speeds have hovered near 3–5 GHz for nearly two decades instead of racing past 10.

Moore's Law — the doubling of transistor count — still limps along, but each new manufacturing node is astronomically expensive and delivers less. The old strategy of "just wait for the next process" no longer pays off. The industry had to find performance somewhere else.

The answer was specialization — and that's this whole course

If you can't make a general chip faster, make a specific chip for the work that matters. First came multicore. Then GPUs escaped graphics and conquered scientific computing and AI. Then TPUs, NPUs, and a wave of custom AI silicon. Computer architects call this a new golden age: performance now comes from co-designing the hardware and the algorithm together, not from a faster clock. The CPU → GPU → TPU progression you just learned is the story of this era.

The Cambrian explosion of chips

Custom AI silicon is everywhere now: Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium, wafer-scale engines, inference-only chips, on-device NPUs. Each targets a slice of the AI workload the general chips leave on the table.

Building up, not just smaller

With shrinking hitting limits, progress moves to packaging: chiplets stitched together, memory stacked in 3D (HBM), silicon glued side-by-side. The chip becomes a system.

★
Why this matters for you The comfortable abstraction — "hardware is a fast box that runs whatever I write" — has broken. Performance, cost, and even what's possible in AI now depend on how well software fits the silicon. That's exactly why understanding pipelines, caches, warps, and systolic arrays is leverage: the people who can reason across the hardware-software boundary are the ones who make models train faster, run cheaper, and fit where they couldn't before. You're learning the right thing at the right time.
◆
A note of honesty "Hardware is the future" doesn't mean software stops mattering — it means the boundary between them is where the value is. The most valuable engineers in this era aren't pure hardware or pure software; they're the ones fluent enough in both to co-design. This course is a first step onto that bridge, not the whole crossing. Keep going: pick a real workload, profile it, and watch these ideas turn into speed.
