00 / Orientation

Statistics is how machines reason under uncertainty

Every model you train is a statistical claim. A loss function is a likelihood in disguise. A softmax output is a probability distribution. Dropout is a Monte Carlo approximation. This notebook builds the statistical machinery underneath AI from the ground up — and instead of staring at static formulas, you'll nudge sliders and watch the math move.

The strip at the top of the page is live: it's drawing real random samples right now and re-computing their mean and standard deviation as they arrive — the same operation every model does internally when it estimates parameters from data. Keep an eye on it as you scroll; you'll meet that exact mechanism again and again, just wearing different clothes.

▸

How to use this notebook: read the short explanation, then play with the panel below it before moving on. The intuition comes from your hands, not your eyes.

The mean is the balance point of the data — it's pulled hard by outliers, which is exactly why a single point dragged far to the right will visibly drag the teal line with it. The median (amber) doesn't care how far an outlier goes, only that it's "above" — drag a point to the far edge and watch the median barely flinch while the mean keeps chasing it.

▸

Why this matters for AI: feature normalization ((x − μ) / σ) is literally this chapter — every input to a neural net is usually rescaled using exactly these two numbers before training even starts.

▸

Why this matters for AI: every accuracy number you report from a test set is a p̂ — an estimate, not the truth. Small test sets give noisy estimates, which is why "we beat the baseline by 0.3%" on 200 examples should make you suspicious.

03 / Foundations

Probability distributions

A distribution is a complete description of every outcome and how likely it is. Switch between the four distributions every ML engineer runs into constantly, and drag their parameters to see how shape responds.

EXPLORER

Uniform — every outcome equally likely (a fresh weight initialization sampled from U(−a, a)). Normal — the bell curve that the Central Limit Theorem keeps producing everywhere. Binomial — counting successes across independent trials with fixed probability (classification accuracy across n test examples). Poisson — counting rare events in a fixed window (requests hitting a server per second).

▸

Why this matters for AI: choosing a loss function is choosing a distribution. Mean-squared-error assumes Gaussian-distributed errors; cross-entropy assumes a categorical/Bernoulli distribution over classes. Pick the wrong one and you're optimizing the wrong likelihood.

A z-score just answers "how many standard deviations from the mean is this point?" — z = (x − μ) / σ. It's the same normalization trick from Chapter 1, repurposed to compare apples and oranges: a z-score of +2 means the same thing whether you're looking at exam scores or rocket-engine temperatures.

P(μ−σ < X < μ+σ) ≈ 68.3%    P(μ−2σ < X < μ+2σ) ≈ 95.4%    P(μ−3σ < X < μ+3σ) ≈ 99.7%the empirical "68-95-99.7" rule — try matching it with the handles above
▸

Why this matters for AI: anomaly detection often boils down to flagging anything beyond ~3σ from a learned mean. Batch normalization layers inside neural networks are explicitly re-centering activations into roughly this shape, layer after layer.

The Central Limit Theorem

Here's the most quietly miraculous fact in statistics: take any distribution — even a lopsided, ugly one — repeatedly draw samples from it, average each sample, and the distribution of those averages converges to a normal curve. Pick a weird population shape below, then start drawing samples.

Why this matters for AI: this is why averaging predictions across an ensemble of models — or across mini-batches during training — produces something more stable than any single noisy estimate. Variance reduction by averaging is the CLT at work.

Covariance tells you the direction two variables move together; correlation rescales that into a unitless number between −1 and 1 so you can actually compare relationships across different variables. r = 1 is a perfect upward line, r = −1 a perfect downward line, r ≈ 0 is the random cloud you'd get from two unrelated variables — including, notably, a perfect circle, where x and y are clearly related but not linearly related.

r = Cov(X,Y) / (σ_X · σ_Y)correlation: covariance, normalized by each variable's own spread
▸

Why this matters for AI: the covariance matrix of your features is the backbone of PCA, whitening, and Gaussian models. And the circle example is a permanent warning label: a feature with r≈0 to your target isn't necessarily useless — it might be related in a way only a nonlinear model can see.

Bayes' theorem

A medical test for a rare disease is 95% accurate. You test positive. What's the actual chance you have the disease? Most people's gut answer is "about 95%." Adjust the sliders below and watch why that's almost always wrong.

SIMULATOR — disease testing
P(disease)
—
P(positive test)
—
P(disease | positive)
—

The bar chart shows 1,000 simulated people split by true disease status, then split again by test result. The posterior P(disease | positive) is the sliver of true-positives divided by all positives — true and false. At 1% prevalence, false positives from the healthy 99% can swamp the true positives from the sick 1%, even with a 95%-accurate test.

P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B)posterior = likelihood × prior, normalized by the evidence
▸

Why this matters for AI: this is the engine behind naive Bayes classifiers, Bayesian neural networks, and every "prior belief gets updated by evidence" framing in probabilistic ML — including why a classifier trained on a balanced dataset can mislead you about real-world performance when the true class balance is skewed.

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval does not mean "there's a 95% chance the true mean is in this range." It means: if you repeated this sampling process forever, 95% of the intervals you'd construct would contain the true mean. Draw a batch of samples below and watch how many intervals actually capture the true mean — shown as the dashed line.

Each horizontal bar is one experiment's 95% CI, built from one fresh batch of sampled data. Bars colored teal capture the true mean (dashed line); bars colored rose miss it. Over enough draws, the hit rate converges to your chosen confidence level — not because any single interval is "95% likely," but because the procedure succeeds 95% of the time in the long run.

CI = x̄ ± z · (σ / √n)sample mean, plus or minus a margin that shrinks as n grows — straight out of the CLT
▸

Why this matters for AI: reporting "92.4% accuracy" with no interval hides whether that number is reliable or a fluke from a small, noisy test set. Bootstrap confidence intervals are a standard way ML practitioners quantify how much to trust a single evaluation run.

09 / Inference

Hypothesis testing & p-values

Suppose a baseline model gets 50% accuracy. Your new model scores higher on a test set — but is that real improvement, or just noise? A hypothesis test asks: "if there were truly no difference, how surprising would this result be?" Drag the slider to move your observed result and watch the p-value respond.

SIMULATOR — is the observed effect real?
p-value
—
α = 0.05 decision

The shaded region is the p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme, if the null hypothesis (no real effect) were true. Slide past z ≈ 1.96 on a two-tailed test and the shaded area shrinks below 0.05 — the conventional line for "statistically significant," meaning the result would be fairly unusual under pure chance.

▸

Why this matters for AI: A/B testing a new model in production, comparing two architectures' benchmark scores, or deciding if a fine-tune actually helped — all of it is this exact test. And it cuts both ways: p < 0.05 is not proof of a real effect, just evidence against "pure chance" as the explanation, especially once you're running many comparisons at once (the multiple-testing problem).

Maximum likelihood estimation

You flip an unfair coin 10 times and get 7 heads. What's your best guess for the coin's true bias? MLE answers this by asking: "which value of p makes the data I actually observed most probable?" Flip the coin below and watch the likelihood curve reshape itself — and its peak track your estimate.

SIMULATOR — likelihood surface for a coin
Flips (H/T)
0 / 0
MLE estimate p̂
—
Log-likelihood at peak
—

The curve plots L(p) = p^heads · (1−p)^tails across every possible value of p — the likelihood of the data you've actually seen, as a function of an unknown parameter. The peak is always exactly at heads / total flips, which is why MLE for a coin reduces to "just compute the observed frequency." For more complex models, that peak has no clean formula — which is precisely what gradient descent is for.

p̂ = argmax_p   P(data | p)maximum likelihood: pick the parameter that makes the observed data least surprising
▸

Why this matters for AI: training a neural net by minimizing cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood estimation — you're finding the weights that make the training labels most probable under your model. Nearly every standard loss function is a negative log-likelihood wearing a different name.

Entropy, cross-entropy & KL divergence

Entropy measures how "spread out" or unpredictable a distribution is. Shape a true distribution and a model's predicted distribution across four outcomes below, and watch how far apart they are in an information-theoretic sense.

PLAYGROUND — drag the bars
H(true) — entropy
—
H(true, model) — cross-entropy
—
KL(true ‖ model) — divergence
—

The violet bars are the true label distribution; the teal bars are your model's predicted distribution. Entropy is the irreducible uncertainty in the truth itself. Cross-entropy is how many bits it costs to describe the truth using the model's (possibly wrong) probabilities. KL divergence is simply the difference: the extra cost paid for the model being wrong. Make the model match the truth exactly and KL collapses to zero — cross-entropy bottoms out at entropy and can't go lower.

D_KL(P‖Q) = Σ P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x))  =  H(P,Q) − H(P)KL divergence: cross-entropy minus the unavoidable baseline entropy
▸

Why this matters for AI: cross-entropy loss is the standard objective for classification — you're literally minimizing the panel above's amber number every training step. KL divergence shows up in VAEs (regularizing the latent space), knowledge distillation (matching a small model to a large one's outputs), and RLHF (keeping a fine-tuned model close to its reference policy).

The bias–variance tradeoff

A model that's too simple underfits (high bias); a model that's too complex memorizes noise (high variance). Slide model complexity and watch training error keep dropping while test error eventually turns back up — the signature U-shape behind "overfitting."

Regime

At low degree, the curve is too rigid to capture the true (sinusoidal) pattern — both errors are high: underfitting, dominated by bias. Push the degree up and the curve starts contorting itself through every training point's noise — training error keeps falling, but test error on unseen points climbs back up: overfitting, dominated by variance. The sweet spot in between is where most of real-world model selection lives.

Expected test error = Bias² + Variance + Irreducible noisethe three components of generalization error, and why you can't drive all three to zero at once
▸

Why this matters for AI: regularization (L1/L2, dropout, early stopping, data augmentation) all exist purely to push a model leftward along this curve — trading a little bias for a lot less variance. Every "validation loss is going up while training loss keeps falling" plot you've ever seen is this exact diagram in disguise.

Monte Carlo methods

Some quantities are too hard to compute exactly — so you estimate them by throwing randomness at the problem and counting. The classic demo: estimate π by firing random darts at a square and seeing what fraction land inside its inscribed circle.

The fraction of darts landing inside the circle approximates the ratio of the circle's area to the square's — and since that ratio is exactly π/4, multiplying the observed fraction by 4 gives an estimate of π. No calculus, no closed-form integral — just sampling and counting, and the law of large numbers from Chapter 2 making it converge.

▸

Why this matters for AI: dropout at inference time, particle filters, MCMC samplers in Bayesian deep learning, and reinforcement learning's Monte Carlo returns all lean on this exact idea — when the true expectation is intractable, average over enough random samples and it converges to the right answer anyway.
